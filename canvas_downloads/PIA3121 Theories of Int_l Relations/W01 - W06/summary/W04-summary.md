### 2.3 Quick and Dirty History of GATT Rounds (Slide 6)
**Keywords:** GATT rounds, tariff cuts, non-tariff measures, Uruguay Round, WTO, Doha

- **Geneva 1947:** $10bn of trade, 23 countries; 45,000 tariff cuts, average 35% cut.
- **Annecy 1949:** 13 countries; **Torquay 1950–51:** 38; **Geneva 1956:** $2.5bn, 26; **Dillon 1960–61:** $4.9bn, 26.
- **Kennedy 1962–67:** $40bn, 62 countries; 35% average cut on industrial goods; anti-dumping commitments.
- **Tokyo 1973–79:** $155bn, 102 countries; 34% cut; first major work on **non-tariff measures**.
- **Uruguay 1986–93:** $3.7tn, 123 countries; extended to **services and intellectual property**; built-in agenda on agriculture; **created the WTO**.
- **Doha 2001–:** 148+ countries; services, agriculture, trade facilitation, development agenda.
- → 23 → 148+ states, tariffs → services/IP: cooperation **deepening over time**.

## 3. The Liberal Challenge to Realism
**Keywords:** Moravcsik, hard-core assumptions, state preferences, complex interdependence, Keohane and Nye, Waltz

### 3.1 Moravcsik's "Three Hard Core Assumptions" of Liberal IR Theory (Slides 13–16) (memorise)
**Keywords:** individuals and groups, subset of society, state preferences, bottom-up theory

- {{A:Moravcsik 1997}} restates Liberalism as a **bottom-up** theory of state behaviour ({{T:Liberal IR Theory}}).
- **Assumption 1 (Slide 14):** the fundamental actors in international politics are **individuals and groups, not states**.
- **Assumption 2 (Slide 15):** states represent only **a subset of domestic society**, not a presumed "national interest".
- **Assumption 3 (Slide 16):** {{D:combined societal pressures determine "state preferences," which in turn shape the state's international behavior}}.
- → Direct rejection of Realism's unitary, rational state pursuing a fixed national interest.
- One-liner: **For Liberals, foreign policy is domestic politics projected outward.**

### 3.2 Preferences Drive Policy: The Cartoon (Slide 18)
**Keywords:** transportation policy, energy policy, foreign policy, domestic preferences

- Cartoon: *"Transportation policy… begets energy policy… begets foreign policy"* — cars → oil → fighter jets (Slide 18).

### 3.3 Keohane and Nye's Complex Interdependence (Slide 17)
**Keywords:** complex interdependence, policy interdependence, costs and benefits, transnational groups

- {{A:Keohane and Nye 1977}}: {{T:complex interdependence}} (also "policy interdependence").
- Definition: {{D:the set of costs and benefits created for specific groups in Country B when dominant groups in Country A try to realize their preferences and values in the international realm by influencing their state's foreign policy}} (Slide 17).
- Significance: this concept **finally convinced most Neorealists they had to engage the Neoliberals and show respect** (Slide 17).
- → Societies are linked through groups, not just governments.

### 3.4 Waltz Doesn't Budge (Slide 19)
**Keywords:** Kenneth Waltz, Neorealism, structural realism, anarchy

- "Neorealist Kenneth Waltz doesn't budge…" (Slide 19).
- Context: {{A:Waltz 1979}}'s {{T:Neorealism}} explains outcomes by system **structure** (anarchy + distribution of capabilities), not domestic preferences.

## 4. International Regimes: Krasner
**Keywords:** international regimes, Krasner 1983, principles, norms, rules, decision-making procedures, constrained states

### 4.1 Definition (Slides 22–23) (memorise)
**Keywords:** converging expectations, issue-area, four components

- Stephen Krasner of Stanford "invents" international regimes: {{A:Krasner 1983}}, ed., *International Regimes* (Slide 22).
- {{D:International regimes are principles, norms, rules, and decision-making procedures around which state expectations converge in a given issue-area}} (Slide 23).
- Four components to recall: **principles, norms, rules, decision-making procedures**.
- Key phrase: expectations **converge** → states can predict each other's behaviour within an issue-area.

### 4.2 Regimes Are Durable — Why Krasner Is Not a Traditional Realist (Slides 24–26)
**Keywords:** endurance, power shifts, constraint, softened anarchy

- Regimes are **more enduring than temporary arrangements** that change with every shift in power or interests (Slide 24).
- Question posed (Slide 25): how does this mark Krasner off as not quite a traditional Realist?
- Answer (Slide 26): **"STATES ARE CONSTRAINED. ANARCHY IS LESS!"**
- → Traditional Realists expect arrangements to track power; Krasner concedes regimes bind states.

### 4.3 Influential Regimes of the 21st Century (Slides 27–28)
**Keywords:** WTO, Paris Agreement, NPT, IMF, ICC, G20, Basel III, Kimberley Process, UNSC 1373, ASEAN

- **WTO:** rules and dispute settlement for **over 98% of global trade**.
- **Paris Agreement (UNFCCC):** climate regime; states set their **own emission targets**.
- **NPT:** stops nuclear proliferation, promotes disarmament.
- **IMF:** **lender of last resort**; emergency loans, financial surveillance.
- **ICC:** prosecutes **individuals** for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity.
- **G20:** largest economies coordinate global economic policy.
- **Basel III:** global **banking regulation** standards.
- **Kimberley Process:** certification against **"conflict diamonds"** funding civil wars.
- **UNSC 1373:** binding counter-terrorism obligations on all states.
- **ASEAN:** central diplomatic hub for Asia-Pacific security.
- → "Anarchy" is densely governed.

### 4.4 "ANARCHY?" and Regimes vs Agreements (Slides 29–30)
**Keywords:** questioning anarchy, agreements, ad hoc, one-shot, facilitation

- Slide 29 repeats the levels-of-analysis diagram, now **"ANARCHY?"** — Neoliberals question how far anarchy actually blocks cooperation.
- {{D:Agreements are ad hoc, sometimes one-shot arrangements}}; regimes are different and more lasting (Slide 30).
- **A key purpose of regimes is to facilitate agreements** (Slide 30).
- One-liner: **Regimes are the standing machinery; agreements are the deals that machinery produces.**

## 5. How Regimes Facilitate Agreements (Slides 31–35) (memorise all four)
**Keywords:** transparency, information, transaction costs, shadow of the future, defining cheating, reciprocity

### 5.1 Mechanism 1 — Transparency and Information (Slide 32)
**Keywords:** monitoring, reporting, data sharing, misperception, security dilemma

- **Monitoring, reporting and data sharing** let states see each other's behaviour and intentions → less **fear, suspicion and misperception**, which fuel the {{T:security dilemma}}.
- {{D:Security dilemma = steps one state takes to make itself secure make others feel less secure, prompting counter-measures that leave everyone less secure}}.

### 5.2 Mechanism 2 — Lower Transaction Costs (Slide 33)
**Keywords:** forums, legal frameworks, negotiation procedures, dispute resolution

- Regimes provide **established forums, legal frameworks and negotiation procedures**.
- *Example:* the WTO — states make deals and resolve disputes without starting from scratch each time.
- → Cheaper, easier deal-making on complex issues.

### 5.3 Mechanism 3 — Lengthen the "Shadow of the Future" (Slide 34)
**Keywords:** shadow of the future, repeated interaction, reputation, long-term cooperation

- Regimes **institutionalise interaction**: states know they will deal with each other repeatedly for the long term.
- {{D:Shadow of the future = the weight actors place on future interactions with the same partner when choosing how to act now}}.
- Discourages short-term cheating; states build a **reputation for reliability**.
- → This is the bridge to Axelrod's iterated game (Section 8).

### 5.4 Mechanism 4 — Define Cheating and Facilitate Reciprocity (Slide 35)
**Keywords:** standards of behaviour, violation, compliance, targeted sanctions, de-escalation

- Regimes set clear standards and rules, **explicitly defining what counts as cheating**.
- Non-compliance is easier to spot; states answer with **targeted, reciprocal actions** (sanctions etc.), not broader escalation.
- → Reciprocity needs a shared definition of defection; regimes supply it.

## 6. Hegemonic Stability Theory
**Keywords:** hegemon, public goods, free-rider problem, hegemonic decline, preponderance of power

### 6.1 Five Propositions (Slides 36–41) (memorise)
**Keywords:** order, public goods, free riding, self-interest, decline

- {{T:Hegemonic Stability Theory}} (HST) — a power-based explanation of open economic order (context: associated with {{A:Kindleberger 1973}} and {{A:Gilpin 1981}}).
- **1. A hegemon is necessary for order (Slide 37):** a stable, open, prosperous system requires a hegemon — {{D:a single dominant state with a preponderance of military and economic power}}.
- **2. Provision of public goods (Slide 38):** the hegemon supplies collective goods all states benefit from:
  - a stable **reserve currency** — *Example:* British pound in the 19th century; US dollar post-WWII;
  - **military security and enforcement** — *Example:* policing sea lanes for freedom of navigation;
  - a **market of last resort** absorbing surplus goods in downturns;
  - a source of **credit and liquidity** in financial crises.
- **3. Solving the free-rider problem (Slide 39):** without a hegemon to bear leadership costs and enforce rules, smaller states would enjoy an open system without contributing → eventual collapse.
  - {{D:Free-rider problem = actors enjoy a collective good without paying for it, so the good is under-provided}}.
- **4. Not altruism (Slide 40):** the hegemon bears the costs because it **benefits disproportionately**; its position on the **technological frontier** lets it reap the greatest rewards from openness.
- **5. Hegemonic decline leads to instability (Slide 41):** when the hegemon's power or will to lead declines, the system becomes **chaotic, protectionist and conflict-ridden**.
- → HST is Realist-friendly: cooperation exists, but because of **power**, not because anarchy has been tamed.

### 6.2 Picturing Hegemonic Order (Slides 42, 54)
**Keywords:** predominant power, authority ties, institutional sites, enforcer

- Network diagrams: a **predominant power** linked to lesser powers through **formal and informal authority ties** and **institutional sites** (Slides 42, 54).
- Slide 54 adds police photos → the hegemon as **"cop"** (Section 7.4).

### 6.3 The Data on US Hegemony (Slides 43–45)
**Keywords:** US share of global GDP, relative decline, China, Europe, multipolar economy

- **US share of global GDP, 1825–2010 (Slide 43):** ~2% (1825) → peak **~35% around 1945** → ~22% (1970s–2000s) → ~18% (2010).
- **Share of global GDP, 1820–2012, PPP (Slide 44):** China 33% → ~15–16%; Europe peak ~40% (c. 1900) → 16%; US 2% → ~19%; India 16% → ~6%; Latin America ~2% → ~8%.

## 7. Game Theory: Why Cooperation Is Hard
**Keywords:** Prisoners' Dilemma, dominant strategy, arms race, Chicken, rationality, enforcer

### 7.1 The Prisoners' Dilemma (Slides 47–48) (memorise)
**Keywords:** confess, stay silent, dominant strategy, suboptimal outcome

- {{T:Prisoners' Dilemma}} (PD): two suspects held separately; each chooses to **confess** (defect) or **stay silent** (cooperate). Payoffs are prison terms, so **lower is better** (Slides 47–48).
- Payoff matrix (A, B):
  - **Both confess:** 5 years each.
  - **A confesses, B silent:** A 2 months, B 9 years.
  - **A silent, B confesses:** A 9 years, B 2 months.
  - **Both silent:** 1 year each.
- {{D:Dominant strategy = a choice that gives a player a better payoff whatever the other player does}} → here, **confess** (2 months beats 1 year if the other is silent; 5 years beats 9 years if the other confesses).
- Outcome: both confess → **(5, 5)**, worse for both than **(1, 1)**.
- One-liner: **In a PD, individually rational choices produce a collectively irrational outcome.**

### 7.2 The Security Dilemma as a PD (Slides 49–50)
**Keywords:** arms race, escalate, limit, mutual restraint

- "Can the security dilemma be modeled as a PD?" — yes (Slides 49–50). Payoff matrix (A, B; higher is better):
  - **Both escalate:** −6, −6.
  - **A escalates, B limits:** 10, −14.
  - **A limits, B escalates:** −14, 10.
  - **Both limit:** 4, 4.
- Dominant strategy = **escalate** → mutual arms race **(−6, −6)**, although mutual restraint **(4, 4)** is better for both.
- → Fear of being the "sucker" (−14) drives both into costly competition.

### 7.3 Chicken (Slides 51–53)
**Keywords:** Chicken game, swerve, crash, US-China, irrational actors

- {{T:Chicken}}: two drivers head toward each other; whoever swerves "loses face", but if neither swerves both crash — the worst outcome for both (standard context).
- Slide 51: **"Imagine the U.S. as A and China as B"** — a standoff where backing down is costly but collision is catastrophic.
- Slide 52: *"But someone didn't behave rationally the night of July 13th, 1950 in Wichita, Kansas"* — photo of a crashed race car.
  - → Game models assume rational players; real actors can miscalculate or act irrationally, so the "crash" can happen anyway.
- Slide 53: **"How to prevent the car crashes of international relations?"** (pile-up photo).

### 7.4 One Answer: The Cop (Slide 54)
**Keywords:** enforcement, hegemon as police, Realist solution

- Slide 54 links the hegemony network diagram to police photos → an **enforcer** (hegemon / "cop") can stop defection and crashes.
- → The HST solution — yet cooperation outlived the cop's decline (Slide 46).

## 8. Axelrod: The Evolution of Cooperation
**Keywords:** iterated Prisoners' Dilemma, shadow of the future, tit for tat, reciprocity, evolutionary simulation

### 8.1 The Iterated Prisoners' Dilemma (Slide 55)
**Keywords:** repeated game, multiple rounds, shadow of the future, population fitness

- {{T:Iterated Prisoners' Dilemma}}: {{D:the same PD played repeatedly between the same players, so today's move affects tomorrow's response}}.
- Slide 55: **"Suddenly the shadow of the future looms"** — screenshot of an evolutionary PD simulator showing strategies' population fitness over generations.
- {{A:Axelrod 1984}}: one-shot PD → defect; repeated PD → expected future rounds make cooperation rational.
- Context: in Axelrod's computer tournaments, tit for tat ({{A:Anatol Rapoport}}) beat all rival strategies.

### 8.2 Tit for Tat — The Best Long-Term Strategy (Slides 56–59) (memorise)
**Keywords:** Hobbesian world, nice, retaliatory, forgiving, reciprocity

- **"EVEN IN A HOBBESIAN WORLD, looking like a PD, the best long-term strategy is: tit for tat"** (Slide 56).
- {{D:Tit for tat = cooperate on the first move, then copy whatever the other player did on the previous move}} ({{T:tit for tat}}).
- Three rules (Slides 57–59):
  - **1. Be nice:** always cooperate at first — **never be the first to defect** (Slide 57).
  - **2. Retaliate, but only once:** if the other defects, retaliate immediately — but only once; **don't be vengeful** (Slide 58).
  - **3. Forgive or persist:** if the other resumes cooperating, resume cooperating; if s/he keeps defecting, keep defecting (Slide 59).
- *Example:* a state honours a trade deal; its partner imposes an illegal tariff; the state answers with one matching tariff; when the partner withdraws its tariff, the state withdraws its own.
- One-liner: **Nice, provocable, forgiving — cooperation by reciprocity, without trust or a central authority.**

### 8.3 The Worst Strategies (Slides 60–61)
**Keywords:** always defect, always cooperate, sociopathic strategy, Jesus strategy

- **2nd-worst: "The Sociopathic Strategy" — always defect** (Slide 60): locks itself into mutual punishment.
- **Worst: "The Jesus Strategy" — always cooperate** (Slide 61): exploited by every defector.
- → Ranking to recall: **tit for tat (best) > … > always defect (2nd worst) > always cooperate (worst)**.

## 9. Neoliberalism's Core Contribution (Slides 62–66)
**Keywords:** evolution of cooperation, selection, war-fighting machines, naïve states, progress

- Slide 62 title: **"Neoliberalism's Core Contribution"** — applying Axelrod's logic to states ({{T:Neoliberal Institutionalism}}).
- The argument in four steps (Slides 63–66):
  - **Concession (Slide 63):** possibly Realists are right that states were **born as war-fighting machines** and still tend toward suspicion because of the security dilemma.
  - **Selection (Slide 64):** over the ages, **states that fail to cooperate are gradually eliminated**; **completely naïve states are eliminated too**.
  - **Survivors (Slide 65):** surviving states prosper through **the benefits of cooperation balanced by a vigorous — yet reasonable — defence of state interests** (tit for tat at state level).
  - **Implication (Slide 66):** **Realists must reconsider several issues** — the world as anarchic today, the **impossibility of progress**, etc.
- → Regimes (Section 5) institutionalise the iterated game.
- One-liner: **Neoliberals accept Realist premises (anarchy, self-interested states) yet derive cooperation from them.**

## 10. The Neo-Neo Debate
**Keywords:** Neo-Neo Debate, absolute gains, relative gains, regime constraint, powerful states, webs of governance

### 10.1 The Neorealist Rebuttal: Absolute vs Relative Gains (Slides 67–69) (memorise)
**Keywords:** flawed presuppositions, absolute gains, relative gains, security dilemma

- {{T:Neo-Neo Debate}}: Neorealists respond that the Neoliberals' core contribution rests on **flawed presuppositions** (Slide 67).
- Neoliberal assumption (Slide 68): "evolution of cooperation" models work only if states seek **absolute gains**.
  - {{D:Absolute gains = gains compared to the past or to what would have been possible from alternative courses of action}}.
- Neorealist counter (Slide 69): because of the security dilemma, states seek **relative gains**.
  - {{D:Relative gains = gains measured against what other states gain}}.
- *Example:* a trade deal gives A +5 and B +10. Absolute-gains A accepts (it is better off); relative-gains A may refuse, since B grows stronger relative to A.
- Slide 69 warning: **"Peace may be passing!"** — if relative gains dominate, today's cooperation is fragile.
- One-liner: **Neoliberals ask "Am I better off?"; Neorealists ask "Am I better off than you?"**

### 10.2 Where the Two Sides Stand (Slides 70–73) (memorise)
**Keywords:** agreement on regimes, power and constraint, ensnarement, undermining anarchy

- **1. Common ground (Slide 71):** both sides agree that **regimes exist and are important**.
- **2. Neorealist position (Slide 72):** **powerful states are relatively less constrained** by regime norms and rules.
- **3. Neoliberal position (Slide 73):** once created, regimes **ensnare all states in webs of governance that profoundly undermine anarchy**.
- → Not *whether* regimes matter but **how much they constrain the powerful**.
