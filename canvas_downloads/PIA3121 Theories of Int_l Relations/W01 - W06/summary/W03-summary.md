## 2. Hobbes: The Pessimistic Baseline (Slides 3–7)
**Keywords:** Leviathan 1651, state of nature, anarchy, security dilemma, early international lawyers

### 2.1 The Argument of Leviathan
**Keywords:** solitary poor nasty brutish short, sovereign, order, prosperity

- {{A:Hobbes 1651}}, *Leviathan*. Slide 3 shows Hobbes's portrait and the famous frontispiece: a giant sovereign whose body is made of many small people, i.e. the state as the combined, authorised power of its subjects.
- **State of nature:** life is {{D:"solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"}} (Slide 4) (memorise the quote).
- **Domestic solution:** the state (Leviathan) **provides order**, and order produces **prosperity and security** (Slide 5).
- **International problem:** there is **no overarching state** above states, only **anarchy and security dilemmas (SDs)** (Slide 6).
  - {{D:Anarchy: the absence of any authority above sovereign states}}.
  - {{D:Security dilemma: one state's efforts to make itself secure make others feel insecure, so they arm in turn and everyone ends up less safe}} (standard definition, Week 2 realism).

### 2.2 Hobbes vs the International Lawyers
**Keywords:** naïve optimism, Grotius, natural law, realist ancestry

- Hobbes is **challenging the early international lawyers**, suggesting their optimism about law between states is **naïve** (Slide 7).
- → Liberalism's founding question follows: *can states cooperate, and can peace last, without a world Leviathan?* Hobbes says no; every liberal on the following slides says "yes, under conditions".
- One-liner: **Hobbes = order requires a sovereign; no world sovereign = permanent anarchy.**

## 3. John Locke: The Early Liberal Optimist (Slides 8–17)
**Keywords:** Two Treatises of Government 1689, social nature, limited state, life liberty property, Law of Nature

### 3.1 Two Treatises of Government (Slides 8–11)
**Keywords:** inherently social, not a security dilemma, social contract, limited government

- {{A:Locke 1689}}, *Two Treatises of Government*; the slide labels him an **"early Liberal optimist"** (Slide 8).
- Three steps (Slides 9–11):
  - **The state of nature is NOT that bad**, because humans are **inherently social**. The state of nature is **NOT a security dilemma** (Slide 9).
  - Individuals still **contract** with each other and with a state **to make things even better**, so government is an improvement, not a rescue from hell (Slide 10).
  - The state must be **limited in its powers**, because its purpose is to **preserve the life, liberty, and property** of individuals (Slide 11).
- → Contrast with Hobbes: for Hobbes the state is a necessary absolute power; for Locke it is a limited trustee of individual rights. This is the root of the liberal concern with **individual rights and constrained government**.

### 3.2 Anarchy as Society, Not War (Slides 12–14)
**Keywords:** Law of Nature, international society, inconvenience, impartial judge

- **State of nature is a society, not war:** against Hobbes's "war of all against all", Locke saw the state of nature **between nations** as a **society** governed by a moral **"Law of Nature"** (Slide 13).
- **Anarchy is "inconvenient", not hellish:** the problem with international anarchy is not constant brutal warfare but the {{D:"inconvenience" of lacking an impartial judge to settle disputes}} (Slide 14) (memorise the word "inconvenience").
- *Example:* two states disputing a border are not automatically at war; the problem is that no neutral court can decide who is right, so each judges its own case.

### 3.3 Natural Law, War and "Realistic Liberalism" (Slides 15–17)
**Keywords:** natural law, treaties, war as specific act, realistic liberalism, prudence

- **Natural law binds states:** states, like individuals, are bound by a {{D:universal moral law discoverable by reason}}, which creates a basis for **treaties and international law** (Slide 15).
- **War is a specific act, not a constant state:** Hobbes saw war as the **default** condition; Locke defined {{D:war as a specific, unjust act of aggression, a deviation from the natural state of peace}} (Slide 16).
- **Basis for {{T:Realistic Liberalism}}:** a middle ground where cooperation is **possible and morally grounded**, but the reality of anarchy still requires states to be **prudent** and **prioritise self-preservation** (Slide 17).
- → Locke concedes anarchy (like realists) but denies that anarchy means war (unlike realists).
- One-liner: **Locke = anarchy is an inconvenience, not a war; states share a moral law; cooperation is possible but prudence still rules.**

## 4. Locke and the International Jurists: Grotius and Pufendorf (Slides 18–23)
**Keywords:** Grotius, Pufendorf, Vattel, natural law, state of nature, regulation of conflict

Locke's relationship to the jurists who founded international law ({{A:Grotius}}, {{A:Pufendorf}}), in five points (Slides 19–23):

- **Agreement 1 — States are in a state of nature:** Locke adopted the jurists' core framework; sovereign nations exist in an **anarchic state of nature** relative to one another (Slide 19).
- **Agreement 2 — Natural law governs all:** this state of nature is **not a lawless void** but is governed by a universal Law of Nature applying to **states as well as individuals** (Slide 20).
- **Refinement — From states to individuals:** Locke's key innovation was to take the "state of nature" concept, which jurists used **for states**, and apply it rigorously **to individuals**, building his theory of government from the ground up (Slide 21).
- **Implicit disagreement — The purpose of the law:** jurists aimed at a **legal code to manage and regulate conflict**; Locke aimed at explaining **why the state of nature itself is flawed** and must be **transcended** (at least for individuals) through a **social contract** (Slide 22).
- **Influence on the next generation:** Locke's **rights-based** philosophy shaped later jurists such as {{A:Vattel}}, shifting the focus towards **state sovereignty and equality** (Slide 23).
- → **Regulate war** (jurists) vs **escape the condition that produces it** (Locke, then Kant). This distinction sets up Kant's attack on the jurists in Section 5.

## 5. Kant: Perpetual Peace (Slides 24–29)
**Keywords:** perpetual peace, moral duty, pacific federation, republics, democratic peace, cosmopolitan law

### 5.1 Peace as a Moral Duty and the Pacific Federation (Slides 25–26)
**Keywords:** abolition of war, voluntary league, no world government

- **Perpetual peace as a moral duty:** Kant moved beyond **managing** conflict; humanity has a **moral obligation** to work towards the **complete abolition of war** and establish a {{D:"perpetual peace": a permanent end to war, not a pause between wars}} (Slide 25).
- **The "Pacific Federation" of republics:** {{D:a voluntary league of free, republican states that agree to resolve disputes peacefully}}; Kant **rejects a coercive world government** (Slide 26).
- → Kant solves anarchy **without** a world Leviathan, which is the key difference from Hobbes's logic.

### 5.2 Democratic Peace (Slide 27)
**Keywords:** republican states, consent of citizens, costs of war, peace among democracies

- {{T:Democratic Peace Theory}}: Kant's core insight that {{D:republican (democratic) states, where the citizens who bear the costs of war must consent to it, are inherently more peaceful, especially towards each other}} (Slide 27) (memorise).
- Mechanism: those who pay in taxes and lives get a say, so they rarely choose war.
- → Revived in the 1990s as the "democratic peace hypothesis" (Slide 54, Section 9).

### 5.3 "Miserable Comforters" and Cosmopolitan Law (Slides 28–29)
**Keywords:** critique of jurists, legitimising war, universal hospitality, travel and trade, interdependence

- **Jurists as "miserable comforters":** Kant dismissed earlier jurists (Grotius, Pufendorf, Vattel) because they merely **codified the rules of war**, which **legitimised war as a tool of statecraft** instead of seeking to eliminate it (Slide 28).
- **Cosmopolitan law and universal hospitality:** {{D:a global law protecting individuals' right to travel and trade freely}}, fostering **interdependence** as another **pillar of peace** (Slide 29).
- Kant's three pillars of peace: **republics** + **federation** + **cosmopolitan law / commerce**.
- One-liner: **Kant = peace is a duty; republics + voluntary federation + free movement and trade make it permanent.**

## 6. The Intellectual Path from Locke to Kant (Slides 30–35)
**Keywords:** inconvenience of anarchy, federative power, social contract between states, moral imperative

Five-step chain (memorise the sequence):

1. **Locke's problem — anarchy's "inconvenience":** with no impartial judge in the international state of nature, **peace is fragile even with a governing moral law** (Slide 31).
2. **Locke's solution — pragmatic and incomplete:** for states, prudent self-interest through **treaties and alliances**, i.e. the {{D:"federative power": the state's power to conduct external relations, make treaties and alliances, and decide war and peace}}. But he offered **no way for nations to exit the state of nature** as individuals could (Slide 32).
3. **Kant takes up the challenge:** he starts from Locke's problem, finds the pragmatic solution **insufficient**, and seeks a **systematic, permanent** solution to the "inconvenience" of anarchy (Slide 33).
4. **Kant's solution — a contract between states:** the Pacific Federation is essentially a {{D:social contract for nations}}, applying Locke's **domestic** solution to the **international** realm; states **voluntarily cede their right to wage war** (Slide 34).
5. **From natural law to moral imperative:** Kant turns Locke's rational Law of Nature into an **absolute moral duty**; perpetual peace becomes **the ultimate goal of political reason**, not just a good idea (Slide 35).

- → Locke lets individuals leave the state of nature by contract; Kant lets **states** leave it the same way.

## 7. Smith and Ricardo: Commercial Liberalism (Slides 36–41)
**Keywords:** harmony of interests, invisible hand, absolute advantage, comparative advantage, anti-mercantilism, trade vs empire

### 7.1 Adam Smith (Slides 37–38)
**Keywords:** harmony of interests, invisible hand, mercantilism, specialisation

- {{T:Harmony of Interests}} ({{A:Smith}}): as with the "invisible hand" in domestic markets, {{D:nations pursuing their economic self-interest through free trade unintentionally create a more peaceful and prosperous international order}} (Slide 37).
- **Absolute advantage and anti-mercantilism** ({{A:Smith}}): attacked the realist/mercantilist view that **wealth is finite** and best gained by **conquest or hoarding** (Slide 38).
  - {{D:Mercantilism: the doctrine that national wealth and power come from accumulating gold and running trade surpluses, so one state's gain is another's loss (zero-sum)}} (standard definition).
  - {{D:Absolute advantage: a nation should specialise in what it produces most efficiently and trade for the rest, so all nations get richer}}.

### 7.2 David Ricardo (Slide 39)
**Keywords:** comparative advantage, mutual gain, cooperation over conflict

- {{T:Comparative Advantage}} ({{A:Ricardo}}): {{D:free trade is mutually beneficial even when one nation is more productive in every good, as long as each specialises in what it gives up least to produce}} (Slide 39) (memorise).
- Stronger case for free trade than Smith's, giving a compelling **economic logic for cooperation over conflict**.
- *Example:* standard textbook case: Portugal is better than England at both wine and cloth, but is *relatively* better at wine; if Portugal makes wine and England cloth and they trade, both end up with more.

### 7.3 Trade Is Cheaper than Empire (Slides 40–41)
**Keywords:** cost of conquest, interdependence, costs of war, commercial liberalism

- **Trade is cheaper than empire:** it is **more profitable to trade** for resources than to **conquer and rule** the territories holding them, which removes a primary historical justification for war (Slide 40).
- **Foundation of {{T:Commercial Liberalism}}:** {{D:deep economic interdependence and an open trading system raise the costs of war so high that war becomes an irrational choice for modern states}} (Slide 41) (memorise).
- → Realism/mercantilism treats wealth as **zero-sum** (relative gains); commercial liberalism treats it as **positive-sum** (absolute gains). This divide returns in the neo-neo debate next week.

## 8. Why It Mattered: Early Modern War (Slides 42–48)
**Keywords:** wars of religion, Thirty Years' War, Napoleonic Wars, famine, disease, displacement

### 8.1 The Death Toll (Slide 42)
**Keywords:** casualty estimates, 1562–1815, motivation for liberal theory

The liberal search for a **permanent** end to war was not an empty academic exercise. Estimated deaths (Slide 42):

- French Wars of Religion (1562–1598): **2–4 million**.
- Eighty Years' War / Dutch Revolt (1568–1648): **600,000–700,000**.
- **Thirty Years' War (1618–1648): 4.5–8 million**, the worst on the list.
- Wars of Louis XIV: about **2 million** for the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714) alone.
- Great Northern War (1700–1721): about **350,000** (military and civilian).
- War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748): about **500,000**.
- Seven Years' War (1756–1763): **0.9–1.4 million**.
- French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1792–1815): **3.5–7 million**.
- → Locke (1689), Smith (1776) and Kant (1795) all wrote in the middle of this sequence.

### 8.2 How People Died (Slides 43–48)
**Keywords:** living off the land, typhus, refugees, razing, long-term decline

- **Killed on the battlefield**: "of course", but not the main killer (Slide 44).
- **Famine and starvation:** poorly supplied armies **"lived off the land"**, stripping whole regions of food, livestock and grain and starving civilians; the **Thirty Years' War** was infamous for this (Slide 45).
- **Disease:** unsanitary camps bred **typhus, dysentery and smallpox**; moving armies spread epidemics among malnourished civilians. **Disease almost always killed far more people than combat** (Slide 46) (memorise).
- **Displacement and exposure:** people fled approaching armies as **refugees** and died of exposure, hunger and violence on the move (Slide 47).
- **Destruction of infrastructure:** razed villages, destroyed crops and disrupted trade routes **crippled communities for decades**, causing further population decline (Slide 48).
- → War's costs fell mostly on **civilians**, which is why Kant's argument that those who bear the costs should consent to war carried moral weight.

## 9. The "Long Peace" of 1815–1914: A Liberal Peace? Nope. (Slides 49–53)
**Keywords:** Concert of Europe, balance of power, conservative monarchies, British hegemony, offshore balancer, WWI, 1913 interdependence

The lecturer's counter-argument: the century of relative great-power peace after Napoleon does **not** show that liberal ideas worked.

- **The Concert of Europe:** not a liberal institution built on a harmony of interests, but an **exclusive club of great powers** (Britain, Russia, Prussia, Austria, France) designed to **manage the balance of power**, enforce treaties, and stop any one nation dominating the continent as Napoleon had (Slide 50).
- **Shared conservative interests:** in the early decades the key members (Austria, Prussia, Russia) aimed to **suppress liberal and nationalist revolutions**, the very movements that might have created Kantian-style republics. It was a peace to **protect monarchies**, not promote liberal values (Slide 51).
- **British hegemony:** the system was underwritten by Britain as an {{D:"offshore balancer": a dominant power outside the continent that intervenes only to stop any continental rival from becoming dominant}}. The **Royal Navy** kept sea lanes open for the trade liberals prized, but was also an **instrument of power** to check rising challengers (Slide 52).
- **The ultimate failure (WWI):** **1913** was a historic **peak of global economic interdependence**. If commercial liberalism were a **sufficient** cause of peace, **World War I should never have happened** (Slide 53) (memorise).
- → The 1815–1914 peace is better explained **realistically** (balance of power, hegemony, great-power management) than liberally.
- One-liner: **interdependence peaked in 1913 and war came in 1914, so trade alone is not sufficient for peace.**

## 10. 20th/21st-Century Western IR Theory: Timeline (Slide 54)
**Keywords:** idealism, realism, transnational relations, neoliberalism, neo-neo debate, end of history, power transition

- **1919–39:** Liberalism, or **"Idealism"**, dominant (League of Nations era).
- **1939–69:** **Realism dominant**, except for a late-1940s interlude; realists mock liberals as "idealists".
- **1970s:** discovery of **transnational relations and interdependence** gives rise to {{T:Neoliberalism}}.
- **1979–89:** emergence of the {{T:Neo-Neo Debate}}: {{D:the debate between neorealists and neoliberals over anarchy, cooperation and relative vs absolute gains}} (topic of Week 4).
- **1990s:** {{A:Fukuyama}}'s **"End of History"**; the **democratic peace hypothesis** (Kant revived); {{T:Constructivism}}; {{T:Critical Theory}}; {{T:Feminist IR Theory}}.
- **2000s–2010s:** **return of Realism**, especially {{T:Power Transition Theory}}.
- → Liberalism and realism alternate in dominance; neoliberalism is a 1970s reaction to **observed facts** (transnationalism, interdependence), not only to ideas.
- Note: "neoliberalism" here is the **IR theory** of institutions and interdependence, not the free-market economic policy of the same name.

## 11. Little's "Three Key Discoveries" and the Realist Picture (Slides 55–57)
**Keywords:** Richard Little, state fragmentation, transnationalism, interdependence, billiard ball model, cobweb model, anarchy

### 11.1 The Three Discoveries (Slide 55)
**Keywords:** neoliberal findings, 1960s–70s, challenge to realist assumptions

{{A:Little}}: three discoveries made by neoliberals in the 1960s and '70s (Slide 55) (memorise all three, in order):

1. **State fragmentation**: the state is not a single unified actor (Section 12).
2. **Transnationalism**: non-state actors and flows cross borders (Section 13).
3. **Interdependence**: states depend on and are vulnerable to each other (Section 14).

### 11.2 Billiard Balls vs Cobweb (Slides 56, 68)
**Keywords:** unitary state, collisions, web of ties, complex interdependence

- **Realist view = billiard balls** (Slide 56): states are hard, **unitary**, sealed units that interact only by **colliding** (force, war, diplomacy). What happens inside the ball does not matter.
  - {{T:Billiard Ball Model}}: {{D:states as closed, unitary actors interacting only through external contact}}.
- **Neoliberal view = cobweb** (Slide 56): a dense, multi-coloured **web or lattice of transnational ties** linking societies, groups and individuals across borders.
  - {{T:Cobweb Model}}: {{D:world politics as overlapping networks of transactions among many actors, not just states}}; the picture behind {{T:Complex Interdependence}}.
- The same image returns at Slide 68 to introduce transnationalism.
- → Each of Little's discoveries breaks the billiard ball: fragmentation **opens the ball**, transnationalism **adds threads between societies**, interdependence **ties the balls together**.

### 11.3 "ANARCHY!" (Slide 57)
**Keywords:** levels of analysis, system level, realist explanation

- Slide 57: a **levels-of-analysis** diagram (individual / state / system, from Week 1) with **"ANARCHY!"** at the system level.
- Realists explain state behaviour mainly from the **system level**, where anarchy is the single driving condition.
- → Neoliberals pull analysis **down** to the state and society levels (fragmentation) and **across** borders (transnationalism), and argue that the system holds more than anarchy (interdependence).

## 12. Discovery 1: State Fragmentation (Slides 58–65)
**Keywords:** pluralism, interest groups, bureaucratic politics, unitary rational actor

### 12.1 A Fractured Society (Slides 58–60)
**Keywords:** special interest groups, pluralism, domestic politics shapes foreign policy

- Political scientists began portraying US society as {{D:"fractured into jumbles of hundreds of small special interest groups, with incompletely overlapping memberships, widely differing power bases, and a multitude of techniques for exercising influence"}} (Slide 59).
- *Example:* Slide 60, a screenshot of the election game **"The Political Machine"**, where candidates chase interest-group endorsements. → Foreign policy is shaped by **domestic pluralism** and lobbying, not a single national interest.

### 12.2 Bureaucratic Politics (Slides 61–62)
**Keywords:** coherent state, rational actor, agencies, organisational interests

- Next, political scientists chipped away at the realist image of the **coherent state as a rational actor**, because of **bureaucratic politics** (Slide 61).
  - {{T:Bureaucratic Politics}}: {{D:policy is the outcome of bargaining among government agencies with their own interests, not a single rational calculation}} (standard context).
- *Example:* Slide 62, the **US government organisation chart**: Constitution → legislative / executive / judicial branches → dozens of departments and agencies. → The "state" is a **fragmented collection of organisations**, not one decision-maker.
- *Example:* standard illustration: defence, trade and foreign ministries can push different policies towards the same country.

### 12.3 The Key Multi-Part Question (Slides 63–65)
**Keywords:** unified rational actor, variation by country, implications for realism

The likely exam-style question (Slides 63–65) (memorise):

- **Under what circumstances (if any) can the state be considered a unified rational actor?** (Slide 63)
- **Does the answer vary by state (country)?** (Slide 64)
- **What difference does the answer make for Realism, exactly?** (Slide 65)
- Points for an answer (consistent with the slides):
  - Unity is more plausible in **crises and existential threats**, or in **centralised states** with few veto players; less plausible in **pluralist democracies** like the US, with many interest groups and agencies (Slides 59–62).
  - Realism assumes the state is **unitary and rational** (billiard ball); if that assumption fails, realist predictions based only on anarchy and power lose force, and domestic politics must enter the explanation.
- One-liner: **fragmentation = there is no single "state" making choices; there are groups and agencies competing inside it.**

## 14. Discovery 3: Interdependence (Slides 72–77)
**Keywords:** autonomy of states, trade openness, oil shocks, Russian gas, vulnerability

### 14.1 The Claim (Slides 72–73)
**Keywords:** realist assumption, autonomous actors, mutual dependence

- The discovery of interdependence {{D:demonstrated the inadequacy of the Realist assumption that states are autonomous actors in an anarchic world}} (Slide 73) (memorise).
- {{D:Interdependence: mutual dependence, where events or decisions in one country carry costs and effects for others}} (standard definition).
- Standard context from the reading ({{A:Keohane & Nye 1977}}, {{T:Complex Interdependence}}): {{D:sensitivity: how quickly and how much one country is affected by changes elsewhere}} vs {{D:vulnerability: how costly it is for a country to adjust or find alternatives}}.

### 14.2 Trade Data (Slides 74–75)
**Keywords:** exports as share of GDP, trade openness index, interwar collapse

- *Example:* **Exported goods as share of GDP, 1950–1985**: about **8% → 16%**, peaking around 1980 (Slide 74).
- *Example:* **Globalization over five centuries (trade openness index)**: about **20%** in the 1870s–1913, **interwar collapse** to about **10%**, then a rise to about **60% by 2008** (Slide 75).
- → Links back to Section 9: the 1913 peak was followed by WWI and collapse; postwar interdependence far exceeds 1913.

### 14.3 Interdependence as Vulnerability (Slides 76–77)
**Keywords:** oil price spikes, recession, energy dependence, Ukraine war

- *Example:* **US GDP growth vs oil prices, 1970–2000** (Slide 76): oil-price spikes in **1973–74, 1979–80 and 1990** coincide with **US recessions**. Even the strongest state's economy depends on decisions made abroad (e.g. OPEC).
- *Example:* **IMF map of Russian gas dependence** (Russian gas as % of total energy consumption, 2020) (Slide 77): highest in **Central/Eastern Europe** (e.g. Hungary, Slovakia), relatively high in **Austria, Germany, Italy**. Relevant to the **Ukraine war**: dependence became a lever and a vulnerability.
- → Interdependence does **not** only produce peace (Smith/Ricardo); it also creates **vulnerability** that can be used as power. Ties back to the 1913 lesson.

## 16. Liberalism vs Realism: Comparison Points
**Keywords:** human nature, anarchy, actors, gains, peace, cooperation

Bullet-form comparison, built from the week's slides:

- **Human nature / state of nature:** Hobbes, realist: brutish, war of all against all. Locke, liberal: social, a society under natural law (Slides 4, 9, 13).
- **Anarchy:** realist: permanent condition of insecurity and security dilemmas. Liberal: an "inconvenience" that treaties, federations and institutions can manage or overcome (Slides 6, 14, 34).
- **War:** realist: the default. Liberal: a specific unjust act, and abolishing it is a moral duty (Slides 16, 25).
- **Actors:** realist: unitary rational states (billiard balls). Neoliberal: fragmented states plus MNCs, media, NGOs (cobweb) (Slides 56, 59–67).
- **Economics:** realist/mercantilist: finite wealth, zero-sum, conquest. Liberal: specialisation, comparative advantage, trade cheaper than empire (Slides 38–40).
- **Sources of peace:** realist: balance of power and hegemony (Concert of Europe, Royal Navy). Liberal: republics, federation, cosmopolitan law, interdependence (Slides 26–29, 41, 50–52).
- **Test case against liberalism:** 1913 interdependence peak → WWI (Slide 53).
- → Exam technique: for any case, ask *what is the unit, what drives behaviour, and can cooperation last?* Realist and liberal answers diverge on all three.

## 18. One-Page Revision Spine
**Keywords:** last-minute recap, classical liberalism, neoliberalism, Little, midterm

1. **Context:** Week 3 of the realism → liberalism → neo-neo sequence; midterm quiz 13 Oct covers Weeks 1–6; reading {{A:Viotti & Kauppi}} (Slides 1–2).
2. **Hobbes** ({{A:Hobbes 1651}}): life in the state of nature "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"; Leviathan gives order → prosperity and security; internationally no Leviathan → anarchy and security dilemmas; jurists' optimism naïve (Slides 3–7).
3. **Locke** ({{A:Locke 1689}}): humans social, state of nature NOT a security dilemma; contract to improve; state limited to preserving life, liberty, property (Slides 8–11).
4. **Locke on IR:** international state of nature = society under the Law of Nature; anarchy an "inconvenience" (no impartial judge); natural law binds states → treaties and international law; war a specific unjust act; {{T:Realistic Liberalism}} = cooperation possible but prudence needed (Slides 12–17).
5. **Locke and the jurists** ({{A:Grotius}}, {{A:Pufendorf}}): agreed states are in a natural-law-governed state of nature; Locke applied the concept to individuals; jurists regulate conflict, Locke transcends it via social contract; influenced {{A:Vattel}} towards sovereignty and equality (Slides 18–23).
6. **Kant:** perpetual peace a moral duty; voluntary Pacific Federation of republics, no world government; {{T:Democratic Peace Theory}} (citizens who bear the costs consent to war); jurists = "miserable comforters"; cosmopolitan law of hospitality, travel and trade → interdependence (Slides 24–29).
7. **Locke → Kant:** inconvenience of anarchy → federative power (pragmatic, no exit) → Kant seeks a permanent fix → social contract between states, ceding the right to war → natural law becomes moral imperative (Slides 30–35).
8. **Smith and Ricardo:** harmony of interests (invisible hand); absolute advantage vs mercantilism; comparative advantage (gain even if one is better at everything); trade cheaper than empire → {{T:Commercial Liberalism}}: interdependence makes war irrational (Slides 36–41).
9. **Why it mattered:** 1562–1815 wars killed millions (Thirty Years' War 4.5–8 m; Napoleonic 3.5–7 m); most died of famine, disease, displacement and ruined infrastructure; disease killed more than combat (Slides 42–48).
10. **Long Peace 1815–1914 not liberal:** Concert of Europe = great-power balance-of-power club; protected monarchies against liberal revolutions; British offshore balancing and the Royal Navy; 1913 interdependence peak → WWI, so commercial liberalism is not sufficient for peace (Slides 49–53).
11. **Timeline:** 1919–39 Idealism; 1939–69 Realism; 1970s neoliberalism from transnationalism and interdependence; 1979–89 neo-neo debate; 1990s End of History, democratic peace, constructivism, critical and feminist theory; 2000s–10s realism returns, power transition theory (Slide 54).
12. **Little's three discoveries:** state fragmentation, transnationalism, interdependence; realist billiard balls vs neoliberal cobweb; realists explain from system-level "ANARCHY!" (Slides 55–58, 68).
13. **State fragmentation:** US society fractured into hundreds of interest groups; bureaucratic politics undermines the coherent rational state (US org chart, Political Machine game); key question: when, if ever, is the state a unified rational actor, does it vary by country, and what does it mean for realism? (Slides 58–65)
14. **Transnationalism:** MNCs, media, jet travel, cross-border pollution, NGOs; airline traffic to 8–9 trillion km, phone calls surge 1940–70, exports to about 1,300% of 1913 by 1985 (Slides 66–71).
15. **Interdependence:** disproves realist assumption of autonomous states; exports 8% → 16% of GDP 1950–85; trade openness 20% (1913) → 10% (interwar) → 60% (2008); oil shocks → US recessions; Russian gas dependence in Europe → interdependence as vulnerability (Slides 72–77).
16. **Global commons:** natural assets outside national jurisdiction: atmosphere, oceans, outer space, Antarctic; warming about +1 °C, shrinking Arctic ice, crowded orbits; Tokyo framework of transformations, levers and commons domains → cooperative global governance (Slides 78–83).
