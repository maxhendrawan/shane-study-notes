## 2. Core Claims of Constructivism (Slides 4–11)
**Keywords:** identity, rational actor critique, neo-neo rationality, socialization, interests learned, structuration

### 2.1 States Are Not All Alike (Slides 4–5, 8)
**Keywords:** identity, values, perceptions, aspirations, worldview, foreign policy

- Constructivism's opening move: you **cannot assume all states are alike as "rational actors"** (Slide 4).
- States vary significantly in their {{D:identity: their values, perceptions, aspirations, and ways of looking at the world}} — and identity in turn **shapes foreign policy** (Slide 4).
- → Contrast with neorealism, which treats states as "like units" differing only in capabilities (Week 4). Constructivism says the units themselves differ in **who they think they are**.

### 2.2 Which Kind of "Rational"? Neorealist vs Neoliberal States (Slides 5–8)
**Keywords:** relative gains, absolute gains, utility-maximizer, cooperation, neo-neo debate

- Even if states are rational, **whose rationality** — neorealist or neoliberal? (Slides 5, 8)
- **Neorealist state** (Slide 6): every "normal" state is rational, **suspicious, self-regarding, amoral utility-maximizer** determined to increase its power **relative** to other states — even at the risk of inviting costly conflicts.
- **Neoliberal state** (Slide 7): every "normal" state is rational **but predisposed to cooperate** in international society and to smooth over conflicts.
  - Goal: maximize {{D:absolute utility — utility compared with what was accrued in the past, or what might be gained now or in future from a different course of action}} (Slide 7).
- Comparison (memorise):
  - Neorealist = **relative gains**, suspicion, conflict-prone.
  - Neoliberal = **absolute gains**, cooperation-prone.
- → Constructivist point: "rationality" is not a neutral fact; the two neo-schools *impute* different rationalities. Which one a state displays depends on its identity and context.

### 2.3 Interests Are Not Self-Evident: Socialization (Slide 9)
**Keywords:** interests learned, identity acquisition, socialization, introjecting norms, international/world society, domestic culture

- {{D:State interests aren't self-evident — states aren't "born" knowing what to go after}} (Slide 9).
- They **learn** what to go after by acquiring identities through {{D:socialization — introjecting the norms of international or world society and of their own domestic cultures}} (Slide 9).
- → Rationalists take interests as **given** (exogenous); constructivists explain **where interests come from** (endogenous).

### 2.4 Structuration: The Endless Cycle (Slides 10–11)
**Keywords:** structuration, agent–structure co-constitution, Giddens, duality of structure, mutual construction

- Through **day-to-day policies and actions**, states help construct international **and** world societies; these in turn interact with domestic cultures and construct them, in an endless, ongoing cycle (Slide 10).
- {{D:Structuration: the ongoing, mutual construction of agents and structures — agents' actions produce and reproduce structures, which in turn shape the agents}} ({{T:structuration theory}}, associated with {{A:Giddens}}).
- Diagrams (Slide 11):
  - **Left:** actions ↔ resources ↔ norms & values ↔ meaning — all feeding back on each other.
  - **Right — "The Structurated Duality":** agents' **knowledgeability** and **structural principles** constitute each other.
- One-liner: *states make the system; the system makes the states.*

## 3. Wendt's Cultures of Anarchy: Anarchy as an Empty Vessel (Slides 12–17)
**Keywords:** Wendt 1999, anarchy, empty vessel, Hobbesian/Lockean/Kantian, culture, shared ideas

### 3.1 Anarchy Has No Intrinsic Logic (Slides 12–14)
**Keywords:** anarchy, levels of analysis, empty vessel, logics of anarchy

- Slide 12 recalls the levels-of-analysis diagram with **"ANARCHY!"** at the system level — the shared starting point of realists and neo-schools: no authority above states.
- {{A:Alexander Wendt 1999}} (*Social Theory of International Politics*; title slide "Cultures of Anarchy", Slide 13) reinterprets it.
- Key quote (memorise): {{D:"Anarchy as such is an empty vessel and has no intrinsic logic; anarchies only acquire logics as a function of the structure of what we put inside them"}} (Slide 14).
- → Against neorealism: anarchy does **not** automatically produce self-help and power balancing. What it produces depends on what states fill it with.
- Standard shorthand (from {{A:Wendt 1992}}): *"anarchy is what states make of it."*

### 3.2 Three Kinds of Anarchic Structure (Slide 15)
**Keywords:** Hobbesian, Lockean, Kantian, global or regional level

- An international anarchy, at the **global or regional** level, can have one of three structures (Slide 15):
  - {{T:Hobbesian culture}} (after Hobbes's war of all against all)
  - {{T:Lockean culture}} (after Locke's rights-respecting state of nature)
  - {{T:Kantian culture}} (after Kant's perpetual peace)
- → Same anarchy, three different "logics" — the decisive variable is culture, not the number of great powers.

### 3.3 Culture as Shared Ideas (Slides 16–17)
**Keywords:** shared ideas, social structure, culture, material vs ideational, meaning of interests

- Each structure is {{D:fundamentally constituted by shared ideas among member states}}; these shared ideas "make up the subset of social structure known as 'culture'" (Slide 16).
- Wendt accepts that social structures have **material** aspects too, but culture — as shared ideas — is "the most fundamental fact about the structure of an international system, giving meaning and power and content to interests" (Slide 16).
- Slide 17 marks this with 🤯: the claim inverts realism.
  - Realism: material power is fundamental; ideas are secondary.
  - Wendt: ideas are fundamental; they tell actors **what material power means**.

## 4. Roles: Enemy, Rival, Friend (Slides 18–22)
**Keywords:** subject position, role, enemy, rival, friend, role-identity, structural property

### 4.1 The Dominant Role in Each Culture (Slides 18–21)
**Keywords:** subject position, dominant role, three roles

- Each structure centres on a **dominant role** played by member states (Slide 18) (memorise):
  - **Hobbesian → "enemy"** (Slide 19)
  - **Lockean → "rival"** (Slide 20)
  - **Kantian → "friend"** (Slide 21)
- {{D:Subject position: the dominant role that a culture of anarchy assigns to its member states}} (Slide 19).

### 4.2 Roles Belong to Structures, Not to States (Slide 22)
**Keywords:** roles vs role-identities, objective positions, collectively constituted, persistence

- {{D:Roles are attributes of the structures, not of the agents or units — "the objective, collectively constituted positions that give meaning to" subjective self-understandings}} (Slide 22).
- Distinguish:
  - **Role-identities** — held by individual states; they "come and go as individuals [states] take on or discard beliefs."
  - **Roles** — positions within the structure; they "persist as long as someone fills them."
- → A single state can change its beliefs, but the enemy/rival/friend position remains until the shared culture changes.

## 5. The Three Cultures in Detail (Slides 23–28)
**Keywords:** central norms, securitization, sovereignty as institution, security community, non-violence, mutual aid

### 5.1 Hobbesian Culture: Enemies (Slides 23–24)
**Keywords:** enemy, securitization, no recognition of right to exist, unlimited violence

- Question: what are the **central norms** of each culture? (Slide 23)
- In a Hobbesian culture, enemies are constituted by **representations of the Other** — i.e. through {{T:securitization}} practices (Slide 24).
  - {{D:Securitization: presenting an issue or actor as an existential threat, which justifies extraordinary measures}} (standard Copenhagen School usage).
- {{D:Enemy: "an actor who (1) does not recognize the right of the Self to exist as an autonomous being, and therefore (2) will not willingly limit its violence toward the Self"}} (Slide 24).
- "An enemy does not recognize the right of the Self to exist as a free subject **at all**."
- → Logic: war of elimination; no limit on violence; survival is the stake.

### 5.2 Lockean Culture: Rivals (Slides 25–27)
**Keywords:** rival, sovereign autonomy as right, limited violence, sovereignty as shared institution, post-WWII

- States **freely agree** to recognize and accept each other's **sovereign autonomy as a right**, and refrain from trying to conquer or dominate one another (Slide 25).
- Violence or coercion may still occur, but rivals **do not try to destroy** the other or eradicate its subjectivity/selfhood (Slide 25).
- {{D:Rival: an actor that may compete with and occasionally use force against the Self, but recognizes its right to exist and so limits its violence}}.
- When all states in an interstate society recognize each other's right to autonomous actorhood, "we can speak of **sovereignty** not only as a property of individual states, but as an **institution shared by many states**" (Slide 26).
- Wendt's empirical claim (memorise): **most of the world, most of the time, has been Lockean since the end of WWII** (Slide 27).
- → Lockean = competition with rules; war happens, but conquest and elimination of states become rare.

### 5.3 Kantian Culture: Friends (Slide 28)
**Keywords:** friend, security community, rule of non-violence, rule of mutual aid, Western alliance

- As of the late 1990s, Wendt saw a Kantian culture rooted among the **US, Canada, most of Europe, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea** and others (Slide 28).
- It forms a {{T:security community}}: members see each other **not even as rivals** on the most important questions, but as **friends**.
- {{D:Friendship: "a role structure in which states expect each other to observe two simple rules: (1) disputes will be settled without war or the threat of war (the rule of non-violence); and (2) they will fight as a team if the security of any one of them is threatened by a third party (the rule of mutual aid)"}} (Slide 28).
- *Example:* NATO's collective defence clause matches the mutual-aid rule; war between, say, Canada and the US is unthinkable — the non-violence rule.

## 6. From Lockean to Kantian: Four Master Variables (Slides 29–39)
**Keywords:** collective identity, interdependence, common fate, homogeneity, self-restraint, efficient vs permissive cause

### 6.1 The Causal Logic (Slides 29–30)
**Keywords:** efficient cause, enabling/permissive cause, collective identity formation, fear of engulfment

- Four **master variables** can cause transition from a Lockean to a Kantian culture (Slide 29) (memorise): **interdependence, common fate, homogeneity, self-restraint**.
- Two types of cause:
  - {{D:Efficient (active) causes: factors that actively drive collective identity formation}} — interdependence, common fate, homogeneity.
  - {{D:Enabling (permissive) cause: a condition that allows the efficient causes to work}} — self-restraint.
- All four may be present; the more present, the more likely collective identity forms (Slide 30).
- **Minimum condition:** one efficient cause + self-restraint (Slide 30).
- Self-restraint enables states to solve "the fundamental problem of collective identity formation: **overcoming the fear of being engulfed by the Other**" (Slide 30).
- One-liner: *any one push (interdependence, common fate, homogeneity) + the brake on fear (self-restraint) = path to Kant.*

### 6.2 Interdependence and Altercasting (Slides 31–32)
**Keywords:** objective vs subjective interdependence, social dilemma, defect, cooperate, altercasting, internalization

- Interdependence must be **objective** to cause collective identity; "the problem is changing objective into subjective interdependence" (Slide 31).
- **Defection reproduces egoism:** choosing to "defect" in a social dilemma also reproduces the egoistic identities that create the dilemma (Slide 32).
- **Cooperation builds collective identity:** by cooperating, Ego implicitly takes on a collective identity, acting "as if" he cares for Alter — even for initially selfish reasons (Slide 32).
- {{D:Altercasting: by cooperating, Ego signals to Alter "I expect you to do the same in return," casting Alter into a cooperative role}} (Slide 32).
- If Alter reciprocates → Ego's tentative identity is reinforced → further cooperation → over time, **internalization of collective identity on both sides** (Slide 32).
- → Identity is **practised into being**.
- *Example:* post-war Franco-German economic integration (coal and steel) as cooperation that gradually reshaped former enemies into partners.

### 6.3 Common Fate (Slides 33–35)
**Keywords:** common fate, objective condition, "same boat", norm entrepreneurs, epistemic communities, fear of exploitation

- {{D:Common fate: actors face a common fate when their individual survival, fitness, or welfare depends on what happens to the group as a whole}} (Slide 33).
- Must be an **objective** condition to count as a cause; subjective awareness of being "in the same boat" is already **constitutive** of collective identity, not a cause of it (Slide 33).
- Perceptions of common fate may depend on **"entrepreneurs"** and/or {{T:epistemic communities}} who lead in reframing how actors understand themselves; such leadership is not always present (Slide 34).
- **Not sufficient** alone: as with interdependence, actors may fear **exploitation** by others in the collective, so believing partners will show **self-restraint** matters (Slide 35).
- *Example:* a shared external threat or climate change objectively ties states' welfare together; scientists (an epistemic community) can frame it as a shared fate.

### 6.4 Homogeneity (Slides 36–37)
**Keywords:** objective homogeneity, subjective categorization, recategorization, group differentiation, Othering

- Collective identity presupposes members **categorize themselves as alike** along the group's defining dimensions (Slide 36).
- So the causal link runs from **objective homogeneity** to **subjective categorization**: increases in objective homogeneity cause actors to **recategorize others as like themselves** (Slide 36).
- Counter-force (Slide 37): actors may **invent or problematize new sources of group differentiation** to shore up the boundary between the group Self and the Other.
  - This need for difference does **not** have to mean aggression or disrespect ("Othering"), nor block collective identity on other issues.
  - But it **weakens the link** between homogenization and prosocial behaviour.

### 6.5 Self-Restraint (Slides 38–39)
**Keywords:** permissive cause, fear of engulfment, trust, egoism, Realism's source

- Interdependence, common fate and homogeneity raise incentives for **prosocial behaviour**, which erodes the egoistic boundaries of the Self and expands them to include the Other (Slide 38).
- This only proceeds if actors overcome their {{D:fear of being engulfed, physically or psychically, by those with whom they would identify}} (Slide 38).
- That fear is "the source of egoism and 'Realism'" (Slide 39).
- To get past it, actors must **trust** that their needs will be respected and their individuality not wholly submerged by or sacrificed to the group (Slide 39).
- "Creating this trust is **the fundamental problem** of collective identity formation" — especially hard in anarchy, "where being engulfed can be fatal" (Slide 39).
- → Realism, on this reading, is not a law of nature but the product of unresolved fear.

## 7. Constructivism and Military Security: Jepperson, Wendt & Katzenstein (Slides 40–51)
**Keywords:** JWK, Culture of National Security, security environment, international cultural environment (ICE), identity

### 7.1 Security Environments Are Cultural (Slides 40–41)
**Keywords:** material vs cultural, institutional environment, decisive

- Source: {{A:Jepperson, Wendt & Katzenstein 1996}}, in *The Culture of National Security: Norms and Identity in World Politics* ({{A:Katzenstein 1996}}, ed.) (Slide 40).
- Claim: states' security environments are **not just material** (e.g. balance of military power) but also — importantly, even **decisively** — **cultural or institutional** (Slide 41).

### 7.2 Three Layers of the International Cultural Environment (Slides 42–46)
**Keywords:** ICE, security regimes, world political culture, amity and enmity, regions

- {{T:International Cultural Environment (ICE)}} has three layers (Slides 42–46) (memorise):
  - (1) **Formal institutions or security regimes** (Slide 43).
  - (2) **A world political culture** (Slide 44).
  - (3) **Enduring patterns of amity and enmity**, particularly within regions (Slide 45).

### 7.3 Example: Canada vs Cuba (Slides 46–47)
**Keywords:** amity, enmity, threat perception, identity over capability

- "Which country is — to the U.S. — the nice country?" (Slide 47): maps of **Canada** (Mounties) and **Cuba** (Fidel Castro).
- Large neighbouring Canada is a friend; small Cuba was treated as a threat.
- → **Identity, not material power, shapes threat perception** — layer 3 in action.

### 7.4 JWK's Key Novel Argument (Slides 48–49)
**Keywords:** incentives vs identities, constitutive effect

- ICE elements don't just affect the **incentives** facing states to behave in certain ways (Slide 48).
- The ICE also affects the **basic character of states: their identities** (Slide 49).
- → Rationalists: environment changes **behaviour** (regulative effect). JWK: environment changes **who states are** (constitutive effect).

### 7.5 Breakthrough: Beyond the Neo-Neo Debate (Slides 50–51)
**Keywords:** neo-neo debate, neo-neo synthesis, intrinsic actor properties, rejection

- JWK: "We **reject** the prevailing assumption, shared by Neorealists and Neoliberals alike, that the defining actor properties are **intrinsic to states**, that they are all power-seeking and (material) interest-maximizing rational actors" (Slide 50).
- Marked 🤯 (Slide 51): the challenge targets **both** mainstream schools at once.
- One-liner: *constructivism attacks what the neo-neo synthesis agrees on, not what it disputes.*

## 8. The ICE's Three Effects on State Identities (Slides 52–56)
**Keywords:** survival, modal character of statehood, variation in statehood, norms and roles

- Three effects (memorise) (Slides 52–56):
  - (1) Elements of the ICE can affect some states' very **prospects for survival** (Slide 53).
  - (2) Changing ICE elements may change {{D:"the modal character of statehood" — the typical form a state takes in a given era}} over time (Slide 55).
  - (3) ICE elements may cause **variation in the character of statehood within one international society**: states may be similar in structure and function but **compelled by norms to play different roles** (Slide 56).
- World political map of today's sovereign states (Slide 54):
  - → Under a Lockean sovereignty norm, even small, weak states survive; contrast the empire maps of 1815 and 1900 (Section 9).
- *Example (effect 2):* the shift from empires to nation-states after decolonization.

## 9. Case: Six Primary Norms of Imperialism (Slides 57–67)
**Keywords:** imperialism, civilized/uncivilized, legitimate conquest, balance of power, mercantilism, non-interference

### 9.1 Imperial Norms 1–3 (Slides 57–61)
**Keywords:** standard of civilization, rule over foreigners, accumulating a domain

- Map "The World in 1900" with flags of the era: most of Africa and Asia under European empires (Slide 58).
- Six norms of the imperial ICE (memorise):
  - (1) **The world is divided into civilized and uncivilized realms** (Slide 59).
  - (2) **Ruling over foreigners was acceptable** (Slide 60).
  - (3) **Accumulating a domain was a legitimate state pursuit** (Slide 61).

### 9.2 US Imperialism (Slide 62)
**Keywords:** Pacific expansion, backyard, 1898, Western Hemisphere interventions

- "US imperialism in the Pacific and in 'its own backyard'" (Slide 62):
  - **Pacific:** Alaska (purchased 1867), Midway 1867, Hawaii 1898, Philippines 1898, Guam 1898, Johnston Island 1898, Wake Island 1899, American Samoa 1899.
  - **Caribbean:** Puerto Rico 1898, Cuba naval bases 1898.
  - **Involvement in:** Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Panama, Santo Domingo, Venezuela; Boxer Rebellion (China) 1900.
- → Even a state with an anti-colonial founding identity followed the imperial norms of its era.

### 9.3 Imperial Norms 4–6 (Slides 63–67)
**Keywords:** balance of power, mercantilism, colonial non-interference

  - (4) **Power should be balanced** (Slide 63).
  - (5) **Mercantilist economic policies were legitimate** (Slide 65).
  - (6) **States should not interfere in other states' colonial administration** (Slide 66).
- World map of 1815: Russian, Qing, Ottoman and British empires; Spanish/Portuguese America (Slide 64).
- "The World in 1900" map repeated (Slide 67).
- → Conquest was once **legitimate** because the ICE said so; today it is illegitimate under sovereignty norms (contrast Slide 54 with Slides 58, 64, 67).
- → Illustrates ICE effect 2: the **modal character of statehood** shifted from empire to sovereign nation-state.
- *Example:* the Ukraine war (Slide 3) is condemned as a violation of the post-WWII norm against conquest — a norm that did not exist in 1900.

## 10. Challenges to Constructivism (Slides 68–72)
**Keywords:** realist critique, rationalist critique, problem of change, "good norms" problem

- Four critiques (memorise) (Slides 68–72):
- **1. Realist critique** (Slide 69): does constructivism pay enough attention to **hard military and economic power**? Realists: norms are nice, but **secondary to material capabilities and survival instincts**.
- **2. Rationalist critique** (Slide 70): identity and culture are **too vague and hard to measure**; neoliberal and neorealist critics say constructivism **lacks the predictive power** of rational-choice models.
- **3. Problem of change** (Slide 71): strong at explaining how norms create **stability** and constrain actors; weaker at explaining **where norms come from** or **why they sometimes collapse suddenly**.
- **4. "Good norms" problem** (Slide 72): it explains the spread of both **good** norms (human rights) and **bad** norms (imperialism, slavery) — a tool for understanding **process**, not for assessing the **moral content** of ideas.
