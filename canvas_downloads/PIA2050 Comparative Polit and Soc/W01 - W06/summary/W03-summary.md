## 2. Recap: The Three Traditions
**Keywords:** Lichbach & Zuckerman, structuralist, culturalist, rationalist, inevitability, agency

- Last week: {{A:Lichbach & Zuckerman 1997}} on the three main approaches — **structuralist, culturalist, rationalist** (Slide 2). Know each as nutshell → strength → weakness.

### 2.1 Structuralists (Slide 3)
**Keywords:** material conditions, relations of production, class structure, constraints

- **Nutshell:** {{D:a country's social and political organisation is determined by material conditions — relations of production, class structure/inequality, technologies}}; systems change through **structural change**.
- **Strength:** history is made in circumstances **beyond the control of any individual**, who face crucial **constraints** in prevailing structures.
- **Weakness:** underestimates **individual agency**; overstates the **"inevitability"** of outcomes.
- → Structure explains the constraints, not the choices made within them.

### 2.2 Culturalists (Slide 4)
**Keywords:** culture, norms, logic of appropriateness, fitting in, no mechanism of change

- **Nutshell:** {{D:a country's social and political organisation is determined by its culture, which shapes the rules/norms of politics and beliefs about authority and institutions}}; systems change through **cultural change**.
- **Strength:** {{T:the logic of appropriateness}} — behaviour motivated by the desire to **"fit in"** — explains much individual behaviour.
- **Weakness:** understates **intentionality**; overstates **inevitability**; **no mechanism for cultural change = no mechanism for social and political change** (memorise).

### 2.3 Rationalists (Slide 5)
**Keywords:** self-interest, political entrepreneurs, collective action problem, unrealistic assumptions

- **Nutshell:** {{D:a country's social and political organisation is determined by individuals pursuing their self-interest}}.
- Outcomes hinge on whether and how leaders of organised groups ({{D:"political entrepreneurs"}}) solve the {{T:collective action problem}}.
- **Strength:** focus on **individual agency** and **intentionality**.
- **Weakness:** **incomplete view of motivations**; **unrealistic assumptions** about how people form beliefs and decide.
- → Two traditions overstate inevitability; the third over-simplifies the individual. This week's framework keeps agency but fixes the psychology.

## 3. Defining Political Development
**Keywords:** progress, change, Human Development Index, Huntington, Fukuyama, institutions, decay

### 3.1 Development as Progress (Slides 6–7)
**Keywords:** potential realised, economic development, UNDP, HDI, rule of law

- "Development" has **at least two meanings** (Slide 6).
- **(1) As progress:** {{D:the process by which something becomes more advanced, or its potential is realised in some way}}.
  - "Economic development" = a poor, non-industrialised country becoming wealthy, industrialised, technologically advanced.
  - *Example:* UNDP's {{T:Human Development Index}}, built from **per capita income, life expectancy, and education**.
- Slide 7 quotation: **"A government of laws, and not men"** — {{A:John Adams}}, *Novanglus Papers, No. 7*: the classic image of political "progress" toward impersonal rule of law.
- → This meaning is normative: it presumes a better end-state.

### 3.2 Development as Change — The Class Definition (Slide 8)
**Keywords:** change over time, any direction, emerge, evolve, decay

- **(2) As change:** change over time, **in any direction** — the definition the class adopts.
- {{D:Political development is the process by which political systems change over time}} (memorise).
- Close to {{A:Huntington 1965}} and {{A:Fukuyama 2014}}, both focused almost entirely on **institutions**. {{A:Fukuyama 2014}} (pp. 7, 23): {{D:"change over time in political institutions" and "the process by which institutions emerge, evolve, and eventually decay"}}.
- → **Decay counts as development too.** One-liner: *development = change, not progress.*

## 4. A Universal History? The "Engine" of History
**Keywords:** universal history, German idealism, Kant, Hegel, dialectic, consciousness, Marx, historical materialism

- Question (Slide 9): is there a **basic mechanism of political development** — an "engine" of history?
- Traced to attempts to write a {{T:Universal History of Mankind}}; in *The End of History and the Last Man*, {{A:Fukuyama 1992}} credits **"the German idealist tradition"** with the earliest efforts.

### 4.1 Kant (Slide 10)
**Keywords:** 1784, cosmopolitan point of view, republican government

- {{A:Kant 1784}}, "An Idea for a Universal History from a Cosmopolitan Point of View".
- Asked "whether, when taking all societies and all times into account, there was overall reason to expect general human progress in the direction of **republican government**" ({{A:Fukuyama 1992}}: 58).
- But **stopped short** of writing a Universal History (p. 59). → Kant supplies the question, not the mechanism.

### 4.2 Hegel: The Dialectic and Consciousness (Slides 11–12)
**Keywords:** conflict, revolution, war, internal contradictions, forms of consciousness, idealism

- Progress arises **not from the steady development of reason** but from "the blind interplay that led men to **conflict, revolution, and war**" ({{A:Fukuyama 1992}}: 60).
- {{T:The dialectic}}: {{D:systems of thought and political systems collide and fall apart from their own internal contradictions, are replaced by less contradictory, higher ones, which give rise to new contradictions}}.
  - Cycle: system → contradictions → conflict/collapse → higher system → new contradictions.
- History is "not only a succession of different civilizations and levels of material accomplishment, but more importantly a succession of different **forms of consciousness**" (p. 62).
- {{D:Consciousness — the way humans think about right and wrong, the activities they find satisfying, their beliefs about the gods, even how they perceive the world}} — has changed fundamentally over time.
- → Hegel's engine is **idealist** and **conflict-driven** — the idea the week returns to in Section 7.

### 4.3 Marx: Historical Materialism (Slide 13)
**Keywords:** materialist dialectic, means of production, surplus, overproduction

- {{A:Karl Marx}}: perhaps the **most famous** Universal History. {{T:Historical materialism}} keeps Hegel's dialectic — systems evolve through conflict exposing contradictions that cause collapse and replacement.
- Replaces **idealism** with **materialism**:
  - Conflict is over **control of the means of production** and **appropriation of economic surplus**.
  - Contradictions are rooted in **material conditions**. *Example:* **crises of overproduction**.
- → Same engine, different fuel: **ideas** (Hegel) vs **material interests** (Marx). Fukuyama later adds **recognition** (7.3).

## 5. Our Framework: Building on Individuals
**Keywords:** microfoundations, instrumental rationality, emotions, visceral factors, beliefs, desires, Elster

- Slide 14: section divider, "Our Framework". Slide 15 restates last week's "alternative" approach as three principles.

### 5.1 Microfoundations and Bounded Rationality (Slide 15)
**Keywords:** aggregate result, realistic assumptions, emotions, visceral cravings, cost-sensitive

- **(1)** All collective behaviour is the **aggregate result of individual actions/decisions**. Explanations need realistic assumptions and {{D:"microfoundations" — mechanisms operating at the level of the individuals involved}} (memorise).
- **(2)** People often do not follow {{T:instrumental rationality}}:
  - **Emotions** can crowd out other considerations such as self-interest.
  - Even resistible impulses — **visceral cravings** — can block deliberation.
  - **Most behaviour is partly visceral but still sensitive to costs and benefits.** (memorise)

### 5.2 Beliefs, Motives and Elster's Template (Slide 16)
**Keywords:** intentional behaviour, desires, beliefs, causes, aggregation

- **(3)** Intentional, goal-oriented behaviour is explained by uncovering agents' **motivations and beliefs**.
- {{A:Elster 2007}}: any social-science explanation takes two steps:
  - **(i) Why individuals behave as they do** — **desires** (what one wants), **beliefs** (esp. about others' behaviour and the effects of one's actions), and **their causes** (possibly **structure/culture**).
  - **(ii) How individual actions combine** to produce the outcome.
- → Structure and culture re-enter as **causes of desires and beliefs**, not direct causes of outcomes.

## 6. Politics, Collective Action and Identity
**Keywords:** organised groups, Olson, emotions, self-image, moral agents, self-categorisation, parochial altruism

### 6.1 Politics as Group Competition (Slide 17)
**Keywords:** collective actors, power, resources, competing visions, group formation

- {{D:Politics is largely the competition between organised, collective actors over the distribution of power and material resources, or competing visions of how society should work}}.
- So we must understand how groups are **constituted**, their **internal functioning**, and their **interactions**.
- Older assumption: organisations arise whenever many people share an interest. {{A:Olson 1965}}, *The Logic of Collective Action*, **challenged** this but **gave no satisfactory solution**.
- Standard context: {{D:the collective action problem — people sharing an interest in a public good are tempted to free ride, so the good may never be provided}}.

### 6.2 Motive 1 — Emotions and Self-Image (Slide 18)
**Keywords:** anger, resentment, fear, guilt, shame, self-worth, fitting in, differentiation

- **Emotions** (anger, resentment, fear, guilt, shame) are impulses to act with **less regard for consequences**.
- More importantly, people care about **self-image and public image**: they seek reassurance of **self-worth and social standing** by **"fitting in"** (esp. with groups central to their identity) or, in other situations, by **differentiating** themselves.
- Result: thinking that is not very rational; action that **contradicts or disregards self-interest**.
- *Example:* joining a risky protest because staying home would feel shameful in front of one's community.

### 6.3 Motives 2 and 3 — Moral Agency and Groupishness (Slide 19)
**Keywords:** Beetham, common interest, promises, self-categorisation, parochial altruism

- **Moral agents:** {{A:Beetham 1991}} — humans have "some notion of the common interest, and acknowledge the binding force of promises they have made"; they do what they think is right even against self-interest. Caveat: **not always a good thing**.
- {{D:"Self-categorization" — thinking of ourselves and others in terms of group membership}}.
- {{D:"Parochial altruism" — favouring people, even unknown people, seen as members of the same group, while discriminating against others}}.
- Linked to {{T:Social Identity Theory}} (lecture video, Slide 19).
- → Three motives (memorise): **emotions/self-image, moral agency, self-categorisation + parochial altruism**.

### 6.4 Sacrifice, Savagery and the Hive Switch (Slide 20)
**Keywords:** altruism–aggression link, perceived threat, manipulation, Haidt, 90% chimp 10% bee

- {{T:Parochial altruism}} plus concern for self-image/reputation makes us capable of **great sacrifice** for "our" group and **great savagery** against perceived external threats.
- Perceived interests and threats **can be manipulated**; the altruism–aggression link is well known (video).
- {{A:Haidt 2012}}'s {{T:Hive Switch}}: people are **"90% Chimp, 10% Bee"**:
  - Most of life = petty individual concerns.
  - Under some conditions we "lose ourselves in something larger" — the self disappears, **collective interests dominate**.
  - Daily, we are "nagged" by a sense of a higher, nobler collective purpose.
- → The psychology of in-group heroism is the psychology of out-group cruelty.

### 6.5 Morality Binds and Blinds (Slide 21)
**Keywords:** moral narratives, political teams, rationalisation, beneffectance

- Categorisation and parochial altruism shape **beliefs** as much as motivations.
- {{A:Haidt 2012}} (p. xxxiii): "People bind themselves into political teams that share moral narratives. Once they accept a particular narrative, they become blind to alternative moral worlds."
- {{T:Morality binds and blinds}}: moral beliefs sustain group cohesion but drive our perception of reality, blind us to **disconfirming evidence and logic**, and produce **snap judgments** we then rationalise.
- Rationalisation is self-deceiving but protects {{D:"beneffectance" — the notion that "I am nice and in control"}} ({{A:Pinker 1999}}: 423).
- *Example:* partisans condemning a scandal by the other party while excusing the same act by their own.

### 6.6 Endogenous Preferences and Collective Identity (Slide 22)
**Keywords:** endogenous, exogenous, rhetoric, narratives, symbols, rituals, dignity

- **Key point:** desires and beliefs are partly {{D:"endogenous" to mobilisation — shaped by what leaders/organisations do to maximise participation}} (memorise).
- Recruits hold pre-existing {{D:"exogenous" desires and beliefs — having external causes}}; group **rhetoric, narratives, symbols, rituals** reshape them and change predisposition to act.
- {{D:Social or collective identities are sources of solidarities, values, and self-images whose affirmation can motivate action}}: "this is **who I am**"; "to defend my **dignity**"; "to earn the **recognition** I/we deserve".
- → The answer to Olson: leaders solve collective action by **shaping identity, emotion and belief**.

## 7. Inter-Group Conflict as the Engine of History
**Keywords:** groupishness, status-seeking, intersubjective recognition, Fukuyama, non-materialist dialectic, dignity

### 7.1 Two Roots in Human Nature (Slide 23)
**Keywords:** social categorisation, group markers, self-evaluation, status-seeking, evolutionary basis

- {{D:Inter-group conflict is the engine of political development/history}} (memorise), rooted in two traits:
- **(i) Groupishness:** social categorisation to define our place; strong emotional attachment to groups; **markers** signalling affiliation; social identities used for **self-evaluation**; parochial altruism.
- **(ii) Status-seeking:** desires for subjective well-being, control of one's social environment, self-esteem, and others' approval — all with an **evolutionary basis**.
- → Groupishness defines **who "we" are**; status-seeking makes us care **where we rank**.

### 7.2 The Struggle for Recognition (Slide 24)
**Keywords:** equal or superior status, laws and policies, recognition defined

- Hence conflict often becomes a {{T:struggle for intersubjective recognition}}: groups seek to have others recognise their status as **equal (or superior)**; concretely, to **change how laws/policies treat groups** according to status.
- {{A:Fukuyama 1992}} gives these struggles a crucial role in history; {{D:recognition is "an intersubjective state of mind by which one human being acknowledges the worth or status of another human being, or of that human being's gods, customs, and beliefs"}} (memorise). Fukuyama video, from 9:50.
- *Example:* civil-rights movements demanding equal legal treatment for a group previously treated as inferior.

### 7.3 A Non-Materialist Dialectic (Slide 25)
**Keywords:** centerpiece, richer than Marx, pride, identities, markers of dignity

- {{A:Fukuyama 1992}}: 144–5 — recognition is "the centerpiece" of a {{T:non-materialist historical dialectic}} "much richer in its understanding of human motivation than the Marxist version".
- **Why richer:** captures "the prideful and assertive side of human nature that is responsible for driving most wars and political conflicts"; allows conflict driven by **ideas** and especially **identities**.
- {{A:Fukuyama 2011}}: 41 — much contemporary politics revolves around **demands for recognition** by groups with historical reasons to feel their worth unacknowledged; demands may have an economic component, but **economic resources are often "markers of dignity rather than ends in themselves"** (memorise).
- → Lineage: Hegel (ideas) → Marx (material conditions) → Fukuyama (**recognition/identity**).

## 8. Social Identity Theory
**Keywords:** Tajfel & Turner, cognitive alternatives, legitimacy, rising expectations, zero-sum, loss aversion, threatened egotism

- {{T:Social Identity Theory}} ({{A:Tajfel & Turner 1986}}), standard context: people draw self-esteem from group membership and seek positive distinctiveness through inter-group comparison.

### 8.1 When Challenges Happen: Cognitive Alternatives (Slide 26)
**Keywords:** hierarchy, inequality, unstable, illegitimate, objective deprivation, consensual status system

- **Hierarchy and inequality** create the *potential* for recognition struggles.
- Subordinates can challenge only if they can imagine {{D:"cognitive alternatives" to the status quo}}.
- Challenges grow likelier when the situation becomes **unstable and illegitimate** — sometimes after an **actual decrease in "objective deprivation"**.
- Conflict is avoided only with a **"consensually accepted status system"** or one **"sufficiently firm and pervasive to prevent the creation of cognitive alternatives"** ({{A:Tajfel & Turner 1986}}: 12).
- → Counter-intuitive: **improvement can trigger revolt**; unquestioned hierarchy can be stable.

### 8.2 Rising Expectations and Zero-Sum Status (Slide 27)
**Keywords:** inter-group comparison, getting begets wanting, intractability, relational value

- Comparisons become **more plausible as group differences shrink**.
- The {{T:revolution of rising expectations}} fits motivation theory's "general rule": **"getting begets wanting"**.
- *Example:* a group gains partial rights and education, starts comparing itself with the dominant group, and demands full equality.
- Once started, recognition struggles are **especially intractable** — identity conflicts are nastier and harder to solve.
- Why: status has **relational value** (its worth depends on others' status), so the struggle is **zero-sum** — "one person's recognition can come only at the expense of the dignity of someone else" ({{A:Fukuyama 2011}}: 41).
- → Unlike money, status cannot be split to leave both sides better off (memorise).

### 8.3 Dominant-Group Backlash and Threatened Egotism (Slide 28)
**Keywords:** loss aversion, status quo, intense discrimination, Baumeister, evil

- People value and fear **losses far more than equivalent gains** ({{T:loss aversion}}), so dominant groups' preference for the status quo should be **more intense** than subordinates' preference for change.
- Challenged groups often "react in an intensely discriminatory fashion" and **redouble** defence of the status quo ({{A:Tajfel & Turner 1986}}: 22).
- {{A:Baumeister 1996}}, *Evil*: some of the worst violence stems from {{T:threatened egotism}} — {{D:violence in reaction to a threat, generally from below, against the self-image of someone with a high (not low) opinion of him-/herself}}.
- → Onset needs imaginable alternatives; intensity comes from the **proud whose status is slipping**.
- One-liner: *recognition struggles start when the subordinate can imagine change, and turn ugly when the dominant fear losing status.*
