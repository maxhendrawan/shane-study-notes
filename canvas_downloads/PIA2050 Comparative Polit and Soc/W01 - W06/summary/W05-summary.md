# PIA2050 Comparative Politics and Societies — Week 5 Summary: A Survey of Regime Types

## 1. Framing: How Nation-States Differ
**Keywords:** nation-state, national content, state capacity, regime type

- **Last week (slide 2):** state-formation and nation-building — why the world is dominated by {{D:"nation-states", i.e. states that govern one nation}}.
- **This week:** how nation-states **differ**, along three dimensions (slide 2):
  - **National content** — do state and nation boundaries coincide? (Section 2)
  - **State capacity** — {{D:the "degree of government", or the state's varying effectiveness in governing/serving a population}} (Section 3)
  - **Regime** — esp. **democracy vs non-democracy** and the **subtypes of non-democracy** (Sections 4–8)
- → Study cue: for any country, ask in order — nation-state or not? how capable? which regime type?

## 2. National Content
**Keywords:** multi-national state, sub-national state, stateness, Linz and Stepan, citizenship

### 2.1 State–Nation Mismatch (slides 3–4)
**Keywords:** dominant form, multi-national, sub-national, contested nationhood

- The nation-state is the **dominant** form of political organisation, but **not the only one**: in some countries the state's territory does not coincide with that of a single nation (slide 3).
- Two mismatch types (memorise):
  - {{D:Multi-national state — a state inhabited by more than one nation}}.
  - {{D:Sub-national state — a state inhabited by less than a whole nation}}.
- Examples are **contested**, because {{D:a nation only really exists in the minds of its members}} (slide 4).

### 2.2 The Stateness Problem (slide 4)
**Keywords:** stateness, stability, suitability for democracy, secession, cleansing, rules of the game

- {{A:Linz & Stepan 1996}}: {{T:the problem of "stateness"}} — {{D:the lack of correspondence between state and nation}} — is very consequential for a country's **stability** and **suitability for democracy**.
- **Stability:** no agreement on **who is a "citizen"**; multi-national states may face **secession** attempts or efforts to **"cleanse"** the polity of undesired minorities.
- **Democracy:** hard to envision a **lasting agreement on the rules of the game** or **commitment to democratic means**.
- → Chain (memorise): **state ≠ nation → dispute over who the demos is → no consensus on rules → instability, weak democracy.**

## 3. State Capacity
**Keywords:** effective, flawed, weak, collapsed/failed, penetration, legitimacy, Siaroff

- States differ in capacity to **control/serve the population**, and not always **uniformly across the territory** (slide 5).
- Textbook ({{A:Siaroff 2013}}, Ch. 3) — four types, most to least capable (memorise): **Effective → Flawed → Weak → Collapsed**.

### 3.1 Effective State (slide 5)
**Keywords:** default category, territorial control, rational-legal bureaucracy, taxation, legitimacy

- Default category: {{D:a state that "controls the national territory and the borders and has sufficient domestic penetration to ensure that national laws and policies are in effect throughout the country"}}.
- Requires a **stable judicial system**, a **rational-legal bureaucracy**, and capacity to **raise taxes** and **provide basic services** (*Example:* public education, public health).
- **Legitimate all over the country and among all social classes.**

### 3.2 Flawed and Weak States (slide 6)
**Keywords:** shallow penetration, corruption, tax avoidance, outside government control, predatory officials

- {{D:Flawed state — "not as effective in terms of depth of penetration or legitimacy"}}:
  - Lower **bureaucratic quality**; more **corruption** and **tax avoidance**; problematic **economic performance**; more **riots and rebellions**.
- {{D:Weak state — "even less effective than a flawed state in that actual areas of the country are outside of government control"}}:
  - Cannot impose national policies everywhere; effect may be limited to **the capital and adjacent areas**.
  - Elsewhere **absent**, or its presence is **illegitimate and ignored**.
  - Officials may **enrich themselves** rather than follow legal obligations; **inadequate for economic and social development**.
- → Flawed = governs everywhere, badly; weak = does **not govern parts** of the country at all.

### 3.3 Collapsed (Failed) State (slide 7)
**Keywords:** anarchy, multi-actor civil war, warlords, frontline caveat, Yemen, Libya

- {{D:Collapsed state — "truly no state authority but rather anarchy, multi-actor civil war, or fragmentation into various autonomous regional areas, often controlled by warlords"}}.
- **Caveat (memorise):** a civil war dividing the country **along a frontline** is **not** collapse if a national government still controls "its" territory and adjacent borders (but not rebel territory).
- **Cases (Table 3.1, end 2021):** only **Yemen** and **Libya** collapsed; **Somalia** by some measures.
  - *Example:* collapsed but since **re-established** — Afghanistan, Angola, Burundi, DRC (Kinshasa), Iraq, Lebanon, Liberia, Sierra Leone.
- → Capacity is a **variable**; collapse is reversible.

### 3.4 The Data: State Strength and Fragility (slides 8–10)
**Keywords:** Siaroff Table 3.1, Fragile States Index, Fund for Peace, capacity vs regime

- **Table 3.1, Levels of State Strength (end of 2021)** (slide 8), {{A:Siaroff 2022}}:
  - **Effective:** e.g. Australia, Canada, Chile, **China**, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Taiwan, the UK, the US, **Vietnam**, the UAE, Qatar.
  - **Flawed:** e.g. Brazil, **India**, Indonesia, Philippines, Russia, Thailand, Turkey, South Africa, Egypt, Iran, North Korea.
  - **Weak:** e.g. Afghanistan, Colombia, DR Congo, Ethiopia, Haiti, Lebanon, **Mexico**, Myanmar, Nigeria, Somalia, Syria, Ukraine, Venezuela.
  - **Collapsed:** only **Libya** and **Yemen**.
- → **Capacity ≠ democracy** (memorise): autocratic China and Vietnam are *effective*; democratic India is *flawed*; Mexico is *weak*.
- {{T:Fragile States Index}} ({{A:Fund for Peace}}; maps on slides 9–10): {{D:a 0–120 score of vulnerability to conflict or collapse; lower = more stable}}. Bands: **Sustainable → Stable → Warning → Alert**.
  - *Example:* most stable (2023) — **Norway 14.5**, Iceland, Finland, New Zealand, Switzerland, Denmark; most fragile — **Somalia 111.9**, Yemen, South Sudan, DR Congo, Syria, Afghanistan, Sudan, CAR, Chad, Haiti.
  - Pattern: the Alert zone clusters in the **Sahel–Horn of Africa–Middle East** belt.

## 4. Regimes: Definition and Map
**Keywords:** political regime, selection method, repression, incumbents, democracy, hybrid, autocracy

### 4.1 What a Regime Is (slide 11)
**Keywords:** formal and informal structure, representation, regime vs government

- {{D:Political regime — "the formal and informal structure of state and governmental roles and processes"}}.
- Includes: **method of selecting** government and assemblies (election, coup, military decision, royal prerogative); **mechanisms of representation**; **patterns of repression**.
- Distinct from: **incumbents**, their **supporting coalition**, and their **policies** (except policies that define/transform the regime).
- → Regime = **rules of the game**; government = **current players**. A new party winning ≠ regime change; a coup ending elections = regime change.

### 4.2 Classification Map (slide 11)
**Keywords:** democracy vs non-democracy, hybrid regimes, authoritarianism, totalitarianism, sultanism

- **Democracy** → liberal / electoral.
- **Non-democracy** → **hybrid regimes** + **autocracy** (authoritarianism, totalitarianism, sultanism; plus post-totalitarianism).

## 5. Democracy: Origins and Core Definitions
**Keywords:** demos, Athens, direct democracy, Przeworski, Lincoln, uncertainty

### 5.1 Athenian Direct Democracy (slides 12–13)
**Keywords:** polis, agora, psephos, idiotes, lottery, metics, exclusion

- {{D:Democracy — more accurately "rule of the demos" than "rule of the people"}} (slide 12).
- Athens pioneered {{T:direct democracy}}, **500–322 BCE**: the demos took part in judicial/legislative affairs and could hold public office.
- Citizens met in the **agora**, heard speeches, voted with **psephos** (coloured stones / bronze ballots).
- Good citizen = participates **rationally, without self-interest**; non-participants = **idiotes**.
- **Legacy (slide 13):** sovereignty of the demos + public deliberation → foundations of modern **accountability**.
- **Limits:** offices filled by **lottery**, not elections; **no individual equality**.
  - Excluded: slaves and **metics** (2/3 of population), women, men without property or military service.
  - Demos ≈ **30,000 male household heads of ~300,000 (~10%)** (memorise).

### 5.2 Przeworski and Lincoln (slide 14)
**Keywords:** parties lose elections, institutionalised uncertainty, real choice

- {{A:Przeworski}}: {{D:"Democracy is a system in which parties lose elections."}} → citizens need a **real choice between viable, highly aggregated alternatives**.
- {{A:Przeworski}}: {{D:"The process of establishing a democracy is a process of institutionalizing uncertainty, of subjecting all interests to uncertainty."}} → **rules guaranteeing true competition**.
- **Lincoln:** "Government of the people, by the people, and for the people."
- → Test (memorise): **can the incumbents lose?** If the result is known in advance, there is no democracy.

## 6. Modern Democracy: Criteria and Categories
**Keywords:** polyarchy, Dahl, Schmitter and Karl, electoralism, Fukuyama, liberal vs electoral democracy

### 6.1 Dahl's Polyarchy (slides 15–16)
**Keywords:** representation, elections, suffrage, expression, information, association

- Modern "people" rule only **indirectly, via representatives**. To avoid deciding whether that is truly "rule of the people", {{A:Dahl 1982}} coined {{T:polyarchy}} — {{D:polyarchy, or "modern political democracy"}} — requiring (memorise all seven):
  - **(1) Elected officials** constitutionally control policy decisions.
  - **(2) Free, fair, frequent elections** choose and **peacefully remove** officials; coercion quite limited.
  - **(3) Practically all adults can vote.**
  - **(4) Most adults can run** for office.
  - **(5) Effectively enforced freedom of expression**, incl. criticism of officials, government, the prevailing system and dominant ideology.
  - **(6) Alternative information** not monopolised by government or any single group.
  - **(7) Associational autonomy** — right to form/join parties and interest groups competing peacefully.
- → (1)–(4) = **elections**; (5)–(7) = **freedoms that make elections meaningful**.

### 6.2 Schmitter & Karl's Additions (slide 17)
**Keywords:** unelected veto players, electoralism, self-governing polity

- {{A:Schmitter & Karl 1991}} add:
  - **(8)** Elected officials exercise powers **without overriding (even informal) opposition from unelected officials** — military officers, entrenched civil servants, state managers must not act independently or **veto** elected decisions.
  - **(9)** The polity is **self-governing**, free of constraints from an **overarching political system**.
- (8) guards against {{T:electoralism}} — {{D:the tendency to focus on the holding of elections while ignoring other political realities}}.
- *Example:* elections held but the army can overturn results → fails (8).

### 6.3 Fukuyama's Three Elements (slide 18)
**Keywords:** Origins of Political Order, modern state, Weber, rule of law, accountability

- {{A:Fukuyama 2011}}: liberal democracy combines:
  - **Modern state** — {{A:Weber}}: {{D:"an organization that exercises a monopoly over the legitimate use of violence in a finite territory"}}.
  - **Rule of law** — {{D:law applies to everyone equally and constrains state power rather than enabling it}}.
  - **Accountability** — {{D:ways for citizens to remove governments that do not serve the interests of society}}.
- **Origins (memorise):** modern state first in **China**; rule of law and accountability in the **"West"**.

### 6.4 Nationalism, Sovereignty, Citizenship (slide 19)
**Keywords:** popular sovereignty, equal citizenship, elections, limits on power

- Rule of law and accountability spread **together with popular nationalism** and its ideas of sovereignty and citizenship.
- {{T:Popular sovereignty}} — {{D:political power ultimately resides in or springs from the people}} → justifies **general elections** to choose governments and **reward/punish** officials.
- {{T:Equal citizenship}} → **limits on state power** protect citizens' rights; at its broadest, **equal right of all to participate** in community decisions.
- → Links to Section 2.2: democracy needs an agreed "people" to be sovereign.

### 6.5 Majority Rule + Minority Rights (slide 20)
**Keywords:** liberal democracy, electoral democracy, liberal autocracy

- Formula (memorise): **Democracy = Majority rule + Minority rights** — free, fair, competitive elections plus "liberal" rights protecting minorities.
- {{D:Liberal democracy — meets both criteria}}.
- {{D:Electoral democracy — meets only majority rule}}.
- {{D:Liberal autocracy — liberal rights without majority rule}}; only **one example** was ever offered (commonly cited: British colonial Hong Kong).
- **Debate:** can elections be truly free/fair/competitive **without freedoms of speech, association, assembly**? If not, electoral democracies are not really democracies.

## 7. Measuring Regimes and Hybrid Regimes
**Keywords:** Freedom House, Partly Free, hybrid, transitional, entrenched, autocracy

### 7.1 Five Democracy Indices Compared (slides 21–23, 30)
**Keywords:** EIU, Freedom House, Polity, V-Dem, International IDEA, scales, categories

- Five big surveys of democracy (slide 21) (memorise: who, how often, scale):
  - {{T:EIU Democracy Index}} — annual; 165 countries + 2 territories; **full democracy / flawed democracy / hybrid regime / authoritarian regime**; **0–10**.
  - {{T:Freedom House, Freedom in the World}} — annual; 195 countries + 15 territories; **Free / Partly Free / Not Free**; **0–100**.
  - {{T:Polity}} — annual; 163 countries; **democracy / anocracy / autocracy**; **−10 to +10**.
  - {{T:V-Dem}} — annual; 202 countries and territories; **no overall categories**; **0–1**. Really five indices (electoral, liberal, participatory, deliberative, egalitarian); the electoral index is built into the other four.
  - {{T:International IDEA}} — **biennial**; 165 countries; **democracy / hybrid regime / authoritarian regime** (democracy further divided); **0–1**.
- Maps: V-Dem additive polyarchy index 2025 (slide 22); EIU "State of Democracy" (slide 23).
- **EIU 2024 headline (slide 30):** global average **5.2/10**, declining slightly; **60 countries** under authoritarian rule. Share of world population:
  - **Authoritarian 39.2%**, **flawed democracy 38.4%**, **hybrid 15.7%**, **full democracy 6.6%** (memorise).
- → Indices broadly agree on the extremes but use different cut-points and categories, so always say **which index** a classification comes from.

### 7.2 Freedom House (slides 24–25)
**Keywords:** Free, Partly Free, Not Free, political rights, civil liberties

- Ratings: **"Free"**, **"Partly Free"**, **"Not Free"**, scored on **political rights** and **civil liberties**.
- → Political rights ≈ majority rule; civil liberties ≈ minority rights.

### 7.3 Regime Counts at the End of 2021 (slides 26–28, 34)
**Keywords:** Table 4.1, 195 regimes, liberal, electoral, semi-liberal, closed, Freedom House trends

- **Table 4.1, Democracies and Autocracies (end 2021)**, {{A:Siaroff 2022}}: **195 regimes in total** (memorise):
  - **Liberal democracies — 39** (slide 26): e.g. Canada, Chile, Germany, South Korea, **Taiwan**, the UK, the **US**, Uruguay.
  - **Electoral democracies — 67** (slide 26): e.g. Brazil, **India**, Indonesia, Israel, **Japan**, Mexico, Poland, Ukraine.
  - **Semi-liberal autocracies — 48** (slide 34): e.g. Hungary, Iran, Malaysia, Pakistan, Russia, **Singapore**, Thailand, Turkey.
  - **Closed autocracies — 41** (slide 34): e.g. Afghanistan, **China**, Cuba, Egypt, **North Korea**, Myanmar, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Venezuela, Vietnam.
  - Check: 39 + 67 + 48 + 41 = 195. Democracies 106 vs autocracies 89.
- **Freedom House change maps 2023→2024** (slides 27–28): each country coded **declined 3+ / declined 1–2 / no change / improved 1–2 / improved 3+**; declines outnumber improvements → continuing **democratic recession**.

### 7.4 Breaking Down Non-Democracy (slide 29)
**Keywords:** self-rule, closed vs semi-liberal autocracy, transitional, entrenched

- **Partly Free** = {{T:hybrid regimes}}; **Not Free** = {{T:autocracies}}.
- Textbook only uses **"Closed Autocracy"** and **"Semi-Liberal Autocracy"** (most hybrids are semi-liberal).
- {{D:Autocracy — literally "self-rule"; the rule of any individual or group not accountable to the population}}.
- Hybrids are either {{D:transitional — moving from one regime type to another}} or {{D:entrenched — relatively stable}}; many typologies exist.

### 7.5 Four Regime Types Compared: Table 4.2 (slide 31)
**Keywords:** liberal democracy, electoral democracy, semi-liberal autocracy, closed autocracy, Linz & Stepan dimensions

- {{A:Siaroff 2022}} Table 4.2, built on {{A:Linz & Stepan 1996}}: ten dimensions (parties/elections, pluralism, civil liberties, ideology, mobilization, legitimacy, constraints, accountability, leadership duration, transition).
- **Liberal democracy:**
  - Free and fair competition among 2+ parties; citizens can change the government; strong, open opposition.
  - **Full civil liberties**; many autonomous actors.
  - Ideology stresses civilian authority, rule of law, individualism, minority rights.
  - **Legal-rational legitimacy**; clearly constrained by constitution, courts, rule of law, professional bureaucracy.
  - Leaders face periodic free elections; transfers are smooth.
- **Electoral democracy:**
  - Citizens can change the government, but elections are **sometimes not completely free and fair**.
  - Civil liberties **usually incomplete**; usually no formal ideology.
  - Constrained by the constitution but **only somewhat** by courts and the rule of law.
  - **Tutelary actors** may be shielded from elections.
  - Path to liberal democracy: gradual removal of the remaining barriers.
- **Semi-liberal autocracy:**
  - Usually more than one party, but elections are **not free and fair enough to change the government**.
  - Civil liberties limited; some pluralism, perhaps predating the regime.
  - Legitimacy includes the **illusion of legal-rational authority**; constrained more by society (esp. **independent media**) than by courts.
  - **No true accountability**; elections "confirm" new leaders.
  - Transition: a stable negotiated transition is possible where civil society is developed.
- **Closed autocracy:**
  - One official party or none; **no or next to no civil liberties**; no significant social pluralism.
  - Usually nationalistic; stresses **demobilization**.
  - Legitimacy from **tradition and the national interest**; leader has legally undefined limits but is checked in practice by the bureaucracy, military and business.
  - **Leadership for life** unless overthrown.
  - Transition must first pass through a semi-liberal phase, unless the regime is defeated in war and occupied by a foreign power willing to democratize.
- → Two cut-lines (memorise): **free and fair elections that can change the government** (democracy vs autocracy); **full vs partial liberties** (liberal vs electoral; semi-liberal vs closed).

### 7.6 Entrenched Hybrid Varieties (slide 32)
**Keywords:** pseudo-democracy, competitive authoritarianism, tutelary democracy, delegative democracy

- {{T:Pseudo-democracy}} — {{D:elections without real competition because the deck is stacked against the opposition, plus heavy restrictions on rights}}.
- {{T:Competitive authoritarianism}} — {{D:like pseudo-democracy, but some regional/national domains are open to real competition, where the government can lose}}.
- {{T:Tutelary democracy}} — {{A:Przeworski 1988}}: {{D:"a regime which has competitive, formally democratic institutions, but in which the power apparatus [...] retains the capacity to intervene to correct undesirable states of affairs"}}.
- {{T:Delegative democracy}} — {{D:free/fair elections anyone can win, but governments face little accountability and can trample minority rights without consequences}}.
- Which criterion each fails (memorise):
  - Pseudo-democracy → Przeworski's "parties lose" test.
  - Competitive authoritarianism → passes it only in some arenas.
  - Tutelary → Schmitter & Karl (8).
  - Delegative → majority rule yes, **minority rights no**.

## 8. Autocracies: Linz & Stepan Typology
**Keywords:** Not Free, authoritarian, totalitarian, post-totalitarian, sultanistic

### 8.1 Ways of Being "Not Free" (slide 33)
**Keywords:** personalist, monarchy, military, one-party, theocracy, paper rights

- Ruled by **personal dictators, kings, generals, single parties, or theocrats**.
- Most have constitutions guaranteeing rights **on paper** only.
- {{A:Linz & Stepan 1996}} — {{T:four-fold typology of non-democratic regimes}} (memorise): **authoritarian, totalitarian, post-totalitarian, sultanistic**.

### 8.2 Totalitarianism (slide 35)
**Keywords:** total control, terror, Gentile, Mussolini, mass mobilisation, total ideology

- {{D:Totalitarianism — the state seeks to regulate every aspect of public and private life, including through extensive surveillance and terror}}.
- {{A:Gentile}}: the state gives "total representation of the nation and total guidance of national goals."
- {{A:Mussolini}}: "Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state."
- Features: **complete monopoly of power**; **active mass participation**; an **"all-encompassing" ideology** legitimising and guiding the regime.
- *Example:* communist — USSR (Stalin), China (Mao), North Korea (Kim Il Sung); nazi-fascist — Italy (Mussolini), Germany (Hitler).

### 8.3 Post-Totalitarianism (slide 36)
**Keywords:** incomplete totalitarianism, PRC, demobilisation, decline causes

- Full cases are **few** and gone for decades; USSR, PRC and fascist Italy were mostly {{T:incomplete totalitarianism}}.
- *Example:* the **PRC** became {{T:post-totalitarian}} after the party **de-emphasised ideology**, **abandoned mass mobilisation**, and **relaxed economic control**.
- **Why totalitarianism declined** (memorise):
  - **(i)** Total control of a large territory and millions of people is hard; **modernity** lets inconvenient information in.
  - **(ii)** "Total ideologies" were defeated in **WW2** and the **Cold War**.

### 8.4 Authoritarianism (slide 37)
**Keywords:** limited pluralism, flexible ideology, performance legitimacy, passive obedience

- {{T:Authoritarianism}}, contrasted with totalitarianism:
  - **Power** more constrained and dispersed — possibly **limited pluralism** (political or socio-economic).
  - **Ideology** less important, flexible; legitimacy from **tradition/culture, national security/interest, or economic performance**.
  - **Participation:** **passive obedience**, not active mobilisation.
- Textbook subtypes: **traditional**, **military** (bureaucratic-military), **religious**, **electoral** authoritarianism.
- One-liner: *totalitarianism demands belief and participation; authoritarianism only obedience.*

### 8.5 Sultanism (slide 38)
**Keywords:** personal rule, family plunder, personality cult, hereditary succession, no legitimacy

- {{D:Sultanism — power centred on an individual leader and his family, who amass extensive wealth by plundering state coffers}}.
- Features: **no constraints** on power; **cult of personality**; **all opposition banned**; **fear/surveillance**; **endemic corruption**; **hereditary succession**; typically **no legitimacy**; officials' power comes from **personal ties to the leader's family**.
- Evolved from:
  - *Example:* communist regimes — North Korea (Kim Il Sung), Romania (Ceaușescu).
  - *Example:* party-based authoritarianism — Iraq (Saddam Hussein), Syria (the Assads).
  - *Example:* military authoritarianism — Libya (Gaddafi), Indonesia (Suharto).
  - *Example:* electoral democracy — the Philippines (Marcos).
- → Defined by **personalism and predation**, not ideology; any regime can degenerate into it.

### 8.6 Quick Comparison
**Keywords:** pluralism, ideology, mobilisation, who holds power

- **Totalitarian:** no pluralism; total ideology; active mobilisation; party monopoly.
- **Post-totalitarian:** party monopoly survives; ideology, mobilisation, economic control relaxed.
- **Authoritarian:** limited pluralism; flexible ideology; passive obedience.
- **Sultanistic:** leader and family; personality cult; plunder; hereditary.
- → Classify any case by: pluralism? ideology? mobilisation or obedience? institutions or a family?

### 8.7 Eight Autocratic Subtypes: Siaroff's Table (slide 39)
**Keywords:** traditional, military, theocratic, electoral authoritarianism, pre-totalitarian, totalitarian, post-totalitarian, sultanism

- {{A:Siaroff 2022}}, after {{A:Linz & Stepan 1996}}, splits autocracy into eight subtypes on the same ten dimensions. Distinguishing features (memorise ideology, legitimacy, transition):
  - {{T:Traditional authoritarianism}} (monarchies): anything from no parties to multi-party elections that do not determine the government. **Traditional legitimacy**; leadership for life, then within the royal family. Transition: monarch accepts a **figurehead role**.
  - {{T:Military authoritarianism}}: parties usually banned. Often nationalistic, stressing economic development; **demobilizes** civil society. Legitimacy from claims to act in the **national interest**; leadership indefinite unless the military rotates power. Transition: when the military **loses legitimacy** (e.g. losing a war) or thinks it has restructured the socio-political order.
  - {{T:Theocratic authoritarianism}}: one party or none (anti-regime independents may be elected). **Ideology from a religious text or school**; religious mobilization. Legitimacy from authority to **interpret scripture**; a religious assembly chooses successors. Transition **has never occurred willingly**.
  - {{T:Electoral authoritarianism}}: several parties and real opposition, but elections cannot change the government. Many autonomous actors; stresses **economic growth and social peace**. Legitimacy from the **illusion of legal-rational authority**; prefers to be genuinely popular. Leadership indefinite unless party term limits (sometimes one term). Key step: **one truly free and fair election**.
  - {{T:Pre-totalitarianism / incomplete totalitarianism}}: one party with a monopoly; elaborate utopian ideology; **partial** mobilization. Legitimacy initially from **how it came to power** (elections, independence struggle). Transition: party must accept or be forced to give up its monopoly.
  - {{T:Full totalitarianism}}: one party; **terror** eliminates organized opposition; no pluralism and hostility to organized religion; **no civil liberties**. Utopian ideology; extensive **obligatory mobilization** with expected enthusiasm. Legitimacy from **ideology plus the dictator's charisma**; unpredictable leader. Transition only via a post-totalitarian phase, or defeat and foreign occupation.
  - {{T:Post-totalitarianism}}: party monopoly with the **beginnings of pluralism** (dissidents, market actors); ideology weakened, **enthusiasm replaced by boredom or careerism**. Shift to **performance legitimacy**; leaders constrained by party and technocrats, chosen peacefully by the oligarchy. Transition ranges from collapse to negotiated.
  - {{T:Sultanism}}: weakly institutionalized party; terror; **no ideology**, just glorification of leader and family. Compliance from **fear, rewards and personal ties**; arbitrary rule, no bureaucratic professionalism. Sultan rarely abdicates and must be **overthrown**, and his inner circle may then **hijack the transition**.
- One-liner: *ask what legitimates the ruler (tradition, nation, God, fake elections, ideology, performance, fear) and you can usually name the subtype.*

## 9. One-Page Revision Spine
**Keywords:** recap, capacity, democracy criteria, hybrids, autocracy types
