# PIA3123 Theories of Development — Week 4: Human Development and Sustainable Development

## 1. Framing: Is Development More Than GDP? (slide 3)
**Keywords:** economic growth, national aggregates, well-being, freedoms, critique of the mainstream

- Have we been too focused on mainstream approaches centred on **economic growth and national aggregates**?
- Are we defining development too **narrowly** — reducing it to income and production instead of **people's well-being and freedoms**?
- The week's two alternatives answer that dissatisfaction from different directions:
  - {{T:Human Development}} shifts the unit of concern from the national economy to the **individual and what they can do**.
  - {{T:Sustainable Development}} shifts the time horizon from the present to **future generations**.

→ Arc of the week: from *how much is produced* (GDP) → to *what people can be and do* (capabilities) → to *whether it can last* (sustainability).

## 2. The Basic Needs Approach (slides 4–5)
**Keywords:** ILO, World Bank, minimum standard of living, redistribution, state obligation, intangibles

### 2.1 Origins: ILO, then World Bank (slide 4)
**Keywords:** 1976, employment, one world problem, poorest groups, institutional uptake

- Ideas picked up first by the {{A:International Labour Organisation}} and later by the {{A:World Bank}}.
- {{A:ILO (1976)}}, *Employment, Growth and Basic Needs: A One World Problem*, set out the parameters of the early approach: {{D:basic needs are 'the minimum standard of living which a society should set for the poorest groups of its people'}}.
- Two built-in moves (memorise): the benchmark is **societal**, not universal ("a society should set" it); the target is **relative** — "the poorest groups", not an abstract average citizen.

### 2.2 What Basic Needs Theory Demanded (slide 5)
**Keywords:** necessities, public goods, political participation, agency achievements, growth vs redistribution

- Required **basic necessities, public goods and political participation**, and placed obligations on states — **not entirely appreciated among Third World governments**.
- Needs are both **physical** (e.g. minimum calorie consumption) and **intangible** — what Sen was to call {{D:'agency achievements' — of participation, empowerment and community life}}.
- Verdict: {{D:growth-based models of development are flawed; redistributive models must include both income and intangibles}}.

→ Basic Needs already seeds the capabilities critique: what matters is not merely what is delivered, but what people are enabled to **do**.

## 3. From Famines to Entitlements (slides 6–7)
**Keywords:** famine, natural disaster vs human action, entitlement, command over commodities, rights and opportunities

### 3.1 The Famine Puzzle (slide 6)
**Keywords:** causation, food availability, distribution, entry point to Sen

- Provocation: **What causes famines — natural disasters, or human actions?**
- Entry point to Sen: famines can occur **without any large fall in total food availability**, because what kills is the failure of people's **entitlements** — their ability to command food through the rights and opportunities they hold.
- *Example:* a drought that barely dents national food output still produces famine if the poor lose the work or wages that let them buy food, while others eat normally.

→ Development is about **command over resources and opportunities**, not aggregate supply.

### 3.2 Entitlements and the Capabilities Approach (slide 7)
**Keywords:** Sen, Nussbaum, beyond basic needs, commodity bundles, rights

- {{A:Amartya Sen}} (b. 1933, Indian economist) and {{A:Martha Nussbaum}} (b. 1947, US philosopher) built on — but **moved beyond** — basic human needs, toward the required **entitlements** for developing capabilities.
- {{D:Sen defines entitlements as 'the set of alternative commodity bundles that a person can command in a society using the totality of rights and opportunities that he or she faces'}}.
- Key move (memorise): from *what people need* to *what they can actually command*, given their rights and opportunities — entitlements are **socially and legally mediated**.

## 4. Capabilities: Beings and Doings (slide 8)
**Keywords:** capability deprivation, beings and doings, substantive freedoms, the life they value, beyond commodities

- **Human poverty exists when there is a deprivation of capability** — poverty is not merely low income.
- A person's capability to live a good life is the set of valuable **'beings and doings'** accessible to them:
  - *Beings:* good health, adequate nourishment, literacy.
  - *Doings:* employment or other economic activity, participation, care, movement.
- {{D:Capabilities are substantive freedoms that allow people to be who they want to be and do what they want to do}}.
- Emphasis falls on the individual's **ability to achieve the kind of life they value**, as opposed to the **acquisition of commodities**.
- *Example:* two people with equal incomes but unequal health, schooling, gender norms or political rights have unequal capabilities — income equality is not capability equality.

→ Same income, different freedom: why commodity- and income-based measures are insufficient by construction.

## 5. Development as Freedom (slide 9)
**Keywords:** real freedoms, means and ends, five instrumental freedoms, not a byproduct

- In *Development as Freedom*, {{A:Sen}} redefines {{D:development as the process of expanding the real freedoms the people enjoy}}.
- Crucial formulation: **freedom is both the means and the ends of development** — not a byproduct. *As end,* expanding freedom is what development is for; *as means,* people with freedoms (to participate, trade, claim, organise) drive further development.
- Sen classifies freedoms into five (memorise): **political freedoms** (voice, elections, association); **economic facilities** (markets, credit, employment); **social opportunities** (education, health); **transparency guarantees** (openness, trust); **protective security** (safety nets).
- *Example:* famines are prevented less by food stockpiles than by democratic accountability and a free press — political freedom as a means to protective security.

→ One-liner: **capabilities tell us what a person can do; freedom makes the route and the destination the same thing.**

## 6. Evolution: Gender Justice and the Human Development Approach (slide 10)
**Keywords:** gender relations, gender justice, quality of life, gender-specific measures, UNDP, HDR

- **Gender enters the theory** through Nussbaum, who sought to define the capabilities **"required"** for a high quality of life.
  - Gender was revealed as a **key determinant of the quality of life** → analysis of gender relations and **gender-specific development measures**.
- **Institutional outcome:** Basic Needs + Capabilities + Sen's freedoms laid the foundation for the {{T:Human Development Approach}} → the {{A:UNDP}} **Human Development Report** and Index.
- {{A:Nussbaum (2003)}} is the supplementary reading on this extension (capabilities as fundamental entitlements).

→ Trajectory of the lecture's first half: **needs → entitlements → capabilities → freedoms → institution and index.**

## 7. Measuring Human Development: The HDI (slide 11)
**Keywords:** three dimensions, indicators, life expectancy, schooling, GNI per capita, composite index

The {{T:Human Development Index (HDI)}} combines three dimensions (slide 11):

- **Long and healthy life** — life expectancy at birth → life expectancy index.
- **Knowledge** — expected years of schooling + mean years of schooling → education index.
- **A decent standard of living** — GNI per capita (PPP $) → GNI index.
- Note the deliberate choices: **GNI not GDP**, and **years of schooling, not enrolment** — income is a means, and knowledge is measured as achieved.
- *Example:* two countries with equal income can rank differently on HDI because of life expectancy or schooling — the intended corrective to GDP-only ranking.

→ The HDI is the theory's operational test: are the **people**, not the economy, improving?

## 8. The Millennium Development Goals, 2000–2015 (slides 12–13)
**Keywords:** MDGs, UNDP, World Bank, heterodox economists, targets, baseline, achieved vs missed

### 8.1 What the MDGs Were (slide 12)
**Keywords:** UNDP meets World Bank, heterodox influence, 2000–2015, global targets

- Characterised as **"UNDP meets World Bank"** — the human development agenda merged with the Bank's poverty agenda.
- **Heterodox economists' influence is visible** in the social and human framing.
- Timeframe **2000–2015**, with quantified, time-bound targets.

### 8.2 Scorecard: Achieved vs Missed (slide 13)
**Keywords:** halving poverty, hunger, education, gender parity, mortality, malaria, water, environment

{{A:Our World in Data}} summary of MDG targets — baseline vs target vs achieved final level (slide 13):

- **Achieved:** extreme poverty halved (47% → 14%, target 23.5%); gender parity in education (indices 0.98/0.98/1.01); malaria reversed (158 → 94 per 1,000 at risk); drinking water, 24% → 9% without access (target 12%).
- **Missed:** decent work (62% → 60%, target 100%); hunger (23.3% → 12.9%, target 11.5%, close); primary education (83% → 91%); under-five mortality (90 → 43 per 1,000, target 30); maternal mortality (380 → 210 per 100,000, target 95); reproductive health access (antenatal care 35% → 52%, contraceptives 55% → 64%); HIV/AIDS (3.5M → 2.1M new cases per year, target 0; treatment 3% → 45%); TB (172 → 142 per 100,000, target under 172); sanitation (46% → 32% without access, target 23%); the environmental cluster — sustainable development principles "nearly all deteriorating" and continued biodiversity loss (Red List Index).
- MDG8 (Global Partnership) is excluded — **no easily quantifiable targets** (slide 13 footnote).

→ Pattern (memorise): **poverty, gender parity, malaria and water were met; employment, education quality, health and the entire environmental cluster were missed.** The environment is where the framework failed most visibly — the bridge to Part 2.

## 9. Critiques of Human Development (slides 14–16)
**Keywords:** fetishism of numbers, data quality, representation, neoliberalism with a human face

### 9.1 Fetishism of Numbers (slide 14)
**Keywords:** technocratic measurement, deeper transformation, indicators vs capabilities, ASER, learning outcomes

- The HDI risks {{D:reducing people-centred development to a technocratic measurement exercise, losing sight of deeper transformation}}.
- Sharp question: **do improvements in development indicators necessarily reflect real expansion of human capabilities?**
- *Example:* {{A:ASER 2018}} (Annual Status of Education Report, India) — around **50% of Class 5 students could not read a Class 2-level text; only 23.8% could do simple division**. Enrolment and infrastructure improved; real learning did not.

→ Enrolment is an input indicator; **learning is the capability**. Where the two diverge, the index flatters reality.

### 9.2 Poor Data Quality and Representation (slide 15)
**Keywords:** representative data, regional disparity, outdated data, rosy picture, planetary pressure, Hickel

- India's HDI data is **not representative in certain dimensions** — regional disparities, outdated or coarse data — so {{D:the HDI may give a rosier picture than the lived reality in some regions}}: an aggregate hides the distribution it is meant to capture.
- See also {{A:Hickel (2023)}}, *The limits of the UN's Planetary pressure-adjusted HDI* — an environmental correction to the index, bridging to the sustainability half of the lecture.

### 9.3 Adjunct to Neoliberalism (slide 16)
**Keywords:** structural adjustment, human face, safety nets, softening not replacing, Pieterse

- Charge: human development acted as an **adjunct to neoliberalism** — giving structural adjustment or neoliberal reforms a "human face" (basic needs, safety nets) often meant **softening market liberalisation rather than replacing it**.
- {{A:Jan Nederveen Pieterse}}: {{D:human development risks being reduced to business-as-usual with a human face}}.

→ Critical template reused against sustainable development: **humane outcomes delivered inside an unreformed growth model.**

## 10. Sustainable Development: Definition and Institutional History (slides 19–20, 22–24)
**Keywords:** Brundtland, intergenerational equity, needs, decision-making framework, Rio+20, three dimensions

### 10.1 The Brundtland Definition (slide 19)
**Keywords:** no single definition, future generations, needs of the present, 1987

- Framing fact: {{T:Sustainable Development}} **lacks a single definition** — the central concept is contested from the outset.
- The canonical one (memorise): {{D:'Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs'}} ({{A:Brundtland Commission 1987, 41}}). {{A:Gro Harlem Brundtland}} chaired it.
- Built on **needs** — the concept from the Basic Needs approach — plus a new **intergenerational** dimension.

### 10.2 Needs, Equity and Culture (slide 20)
**Keywords:** productive potential, equitable opportunities, evolving concept of needs, cultural variation

- {{D:'Societies need to meet human needs both by increasing productive potential and by ensuring equitable opportunities for all'}} ({{A:Brundtland Commission 1987, 44}}).
- Two clauses, two theories in miniature: **productive potential** = growth model; **equitable opportunities** = capabilities model.
- Slide questions: how does 'development' contribute to the **evolving concept of needs**? And {{D:'needs' are defined differently in different cultures}} — no culture-free list exists.

### 10.3 As a Decision-Making Framework (slide 22)
**Keywords:** correct not replace, environmentally protective, alternative to binaries

- Sustainable development works as a **decision-making framework**: it would **correct but not replace** the existing international development model, ensure development is **environmentally protective or restorative**, and displace the binary **"environment or development"** narratives that have dominated public and private political discourse.

→ The week's second "human face" move: not overturning the growth model but constraining and greening it.

### 10.4 Rio+20 and "The Future We Want" (slides 23–24)
**Keywords:** UNCSD, 2012, Rio de Janeiro, Earth Summit, outcome document, growth first

- {{A:Rio+20}} — the United Nations Conference on Sustainable Development — was the **third** international conference on sustainable development, held in **Rio de Janeiro in 2012**, following the 1992 Earth Summit and the 2002 World Summit.
- *The Future We Want* reaffirms achieving sustainable development by {{D:promoting sustained, inclusive and equitable economic growth, creating greater opportunities for all, reducing inequalities, raising basic standards of living... and promoting integrated and sustainable management of natural resources and ecosystems}} ({{A:UN General Assembly 2012, para. 4}}).
- Hegemony check: **growth is reaffirmed first** — the environment appears as *supporting* development.

### 10.5 What Is To Be Sustained? (slide 21)
**Keywords:** natural resources, income from resources, production, consumption, unspecified object

- Unresolved question: {{D:what exactly is to be sustained}} — the slide offers four conflicting candidates: natural resources? income generated from natural resources? production? consumption? Sustaining current **consumption** is precisely what is unsustainable for **resources**.

→ Hold this question — it returns in the critiques (slides 30–31) and the conclusion (slide 34).

## 11. The Sustainable Development Goals (slides 25–26)
**Keywords:** 17 goals, targets, three dimensions, balance, universal, action-oriented, government-led

- The {{T:Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)}} succeeded the MDGs from 2015, with **17 goals and their targets** (slides 25 and 28 show the full set).
- General Assembly mandate ({{A:UN General Assembly 2012, paras. 246–7}}):
  - Goals must "address and incorporate in a **balanced way all three dimensions** of sustainable development and their inter-linkages" — economic, social, environmental.
  - They must be {{D:action-oriented, concise and easy to communicate, limited in number, aspirational, global in nature and universally applicable to all countries}} — while "taking into account different national realities, capacities and levels of development and respecting national policies and priorities".
  - Implementation is **government-led**: "Governments should drive implementation with the active involvement of all relevant stakeholders".
- **Discussion task (slide 29):** how closely do the SDGs align with Sen's *Development as Freedom*, and to what extent do they exhibit **coherence** in objectives and actions?
  - Alignment test: Goal 4 → social opportunities; Goal 16 → political freedoms and transparency; Goal 1 → protective security; Goal 10 → inequality.
  - Coherence test: does Goal 8's growth imperative undercut Goals 13, 12 and 10?

## 12. Critiques of Sustainable Development (slides 30–31)
**Keywords:** weak sustainability, growth vs sustainability, inequality, technocentric, ethnocentric, commodification

### 12.1 The SDGs: Weak Sustainability and Incoherence (slide 30)
**Keywords:** weak sustainability, irreconcilable growth, inequality ignored, Salleh, WTO

- Three objections to the SDGs: they **enforce a weak sustainability** (compensating for nature rather than protecting it); they **fail to reconcile growth and sustainability**; they **ignore inequality**.
- {{A:Ariel Salleh}} (feminist scholar): the SDG proposals "are not just unrealistic but **undemocratic**, since the goals are to be realised by growing gross domestic product (GDP), increasing market liberalisation and free trade, as well as according more power to the World Trade Organization."

### 12.2 Four Structural Critiques (slide 31)
**Keywords:** technocentric, ethnocentric, commodification of nature, sustaining development vs developing sustainability, Daly

- **Technocentric** — faith that technology will fix environmental limits.
- **Ethnocentric** — a Western framing of needs and nature imposed globally.
- **Capitalisation and commodification of nature** — nature re-described as capital to be priced and traded.
- {{D:Sustaining development rather than developing sustainability}} — the adjective colonises the noun: the objective becomes keeping the development machine running, not becoming sustainable.
- {{A:Herman Daly}} (former World Bank economist): {{D:'The term sustainable growth should be rejected as a bad oxymoron'}}.

→ Structural point: sustainability is **subordinate to development**, exactly as the social agenda was subordinate to the market in Section 9.

## 13. Paradigms Compared: Where the Alternatives Sit (slides 17 & 32)
**Keywords:** course map, who needs development, how, importance, comparison across paradigms

The paradigm grid (slides 17 and 32) locates each paradigm by three questions — **Who needs development? How should it happen? What is the importance?** Condensed:

- {{T:Modernisation}} — traditional un-industrialised (non-Western) societies; mutually supportive modernisation plus foreign assistance; route to civilisation and freedom from material want.
- {{T:Dependency}} — peripheral countries (e.g. Latin America); equal trade, technology transfer, changed class relations; reduce surplus extraction, pursue self-reliance.
- {{T:Neo-liberalism}} — indebted and post-socialist states reeling from structural adjustment; privatisation, free markets, deregulation, good governance, social capital; spur growth, make globalisation work for the poor.
- {{T:Developmental State}} — East Asian countries; corporatism, entrepreneurial bureaucracy, export promotion; expand into the global economy, preserve national pride.
- {{T:Human Development}} — **vulnerable and marginalised individuals**; meet human needs through better, holistic measures; reach the poorest, prevent 'everyday' violence, secure **freedom to pursue own choices**.
- {{T:Sustainable Development}} — **current and future generations**; balance growth against environmental degradation, long-term planning, consideration of both mankind and the environment.
- **Post-development** — the final row, blank on slide 17: the next paradigm the course turns to.

→ The alternatives change *who* the subject is (individuals, future generations) and *how* it is judged (freedoms, long-term balance) — but neither repudiates the development project itself.

## 14. Conclusion: What the Alternatives Change — and Leave Alone (slides 33–34)
**Keywords:** shift from national priorities, new foundations, private sector untouched, humanise neoliberalism

- Human development theory shifted planners' focus **away from national-level priorities**, introducing new theoretical foundations and new areas of focus.
- It required **states and aid agencies** to adopt different approaches to development.
- But it **left the private sector largely unaffected** — and the same problem recurs for sustainable development.
- Sustainable development as a policy framework is a platform for international discussion of how development should be achieved.
- Yet it has **not fully answered what sustainability truly means** — i.e. what is to be sustained (back to slide 21).
- Closing question: **is sustainable development essentially a way to humanise neoliberalism?**

→ Both alternatives are **reforms of the mainstream**: they change what is measured and who is counted, while leaving the growth-and-market architecture intact.
