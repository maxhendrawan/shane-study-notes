# PIA3123 Theories of Development — Week 1: Introduction to International Development

## 1. Course Framing: What We Are Actually Studying
**Keywords:** contested concept, interpretive pluralism, politics of development, no step-by-step guide, theory as lens

- This course is **not** a step-by-step guide to development, and **not** a survey of every development theory (slide 5).
- It instead asks two framing questions: {{D:what constitutes development, and how we should understand development issues and their politics}} (slide 5).
- Theories are therefore treated as **competing answers to the same questions**, not as a march of progress from wrong ideas to right ones.
- Lecture agenda (slide 3): what are we studying → course outline → assessment → what is development.
- Every theory is examined on three axes (course outline): its {{D:interpretation of development}}, its {{D:target beneficiaries}}, and its {{D:significance}} — what it means, who it is for, why it matters.
- Logistics: Fridays 0900–1150, Term A 2026/27; lecturer {{A:Francesca Chiu}}, office hours by appointment only, no Canvas messages (slides 1–2).
- → **Study cue:** whenever a new theory appears, ask immediately *who needs development, how should it happen, why is it important?* Those questions are the spine of the course.

## 2. Course Roadmap: The 13-Week Arc
**Keywords:** problem-solving sequence, paradigm families, cumulative structure, quiz checkpoints

- Each week supplies one **family of answers** to the three diagnostic questions; later weeks critique or supplement earlier ones (slides 6, 19).
- **W1** Introduction to International Development — what development is, why it is contested, the map of paradigms.
- **W2** The Making of Underdevelopment: Modernization and Dependency — the founding debate, {{T:Modernisation}} vs {{T:Dependency}}.
- **W3** The Market and the State: Neoliberalism and Developmental State — two rival institutional prescriptions.
- **W4** The Alternatives: Human Development and Sustainable Development — people-centred and future-centred challenges to growth-first thinking.
- **W5** Agents of Development — who delivers development (states, IFIs, NGOs, experts, communities).
- **W6** Quiz 1 (covers Weeks 2–5).
- **W7** Politics of Aid (donor–recipient relations, conditionality, neo-colonial critique). **W8** Gender and Development. **W9** Post-development — rejection of development as a Western-centric discourse.
- **W10** Reading Week. **W11** AI and the Future of International Development. **W12** Documentary Screening (basis of the reflection assignment). **W13** Quiz 2 (whole course).
- → **Structure:** Weeks 2–5 are the core theoretical canon; Weeks 7–9 are thematic extensions; Weeks 11–12 apply and extend. Quiz 1 is the *theory* quiz; Quiz 2 is the *synthesis* quiz.

## 5. Study Strategy: What the Quizzes Reward
**Keywords:** understanding over memorisation, comparison table, apply to policy, relations between ideas

Explicit guidance from lecture and course outline (slide 11):

- The quiz assesses **understanding of main concepts and debates**, not memorisation of reading details; you must **explain main ideas in your own words** and recognise how different theories approach the same issue.
- Recommended method — build a **comparison table**, answering six questions for each theory:
  - How does it understand development **and underdevelopment**?
  - What are its **main assumptions**?
  - What **role** does it give to the state, markets, international organisations and other agents?
  - What **policies** might follow?
  - What are its **main criticisms**?
  - How does it **differ from the other approaches** studied?
- Apply theories to real policies — with the key warning: **do not assume one policy = one theory** (slide 11).
- *Example of the applied style:* if a country protects selected industries and directs investment toward strategic sectors, which approach explains this? (Answer: {{T:Developmental State}} — Section 8.4.)
- → "What does this theory say?" is only half the question. The other half is *why it says this, how it differs from others, and how it reads a real development policy.*

## 6. The Three Diagnostic Questions
**Keywords:** who needs development, how should it happen, why does it matter, target beneficiary, presuppositions

Three questions every development theory must answer (slides 15–17) — the analytical grid for the whole semester. The block opens with a **word-cloud activity** on what "development" brings to mind (slide 4) and closes with a paired exercise to write your own question about international development (slide 20) — both designed to surface assumptions you already hold.

### 6.1 Question 1 — Who Needs Development? (slide 15)
**Keywords:** target population, the undeveloped other, comparative framing, diagnosis

- Asks **who is the subject and beneficiary** of development; the answer implicitly names a **deficit** — a population defined by what it lacks.
- Presupposes a comparison: some other place is already "developed" and serves as the reference point.
- *Examples (slide 18):* non-Western traditional societies; Latin American peripheries; indebted post-socialist states; East Asian countries; vulnerable individuals; present and future generations; subaltern actors.
- → The *same* world contains all these answers at once — the disagreement is about **who is the problem case**, not about the facts.

### 6.2 Question 2 — How Should Development Happen? (slide 16)
**Keywords:** means, growth, equality, freedom, sustainability, contested prescription

- Asks about the **means and mechanism**: what must actually be done.
- Slide 16 stages four rival answers as a live vote, each seeding a paradigm family:
  - "It's all about **national economic growth**!" → growth-first, {{T:Modernisation}} lineage.
  - "Development is about **equality**!" → structural/redistributive, {{T:Dependency}} lineage.
  - "But how about **freedoms and human rights**?" → {{T:Human Development}} lineage.
  - "We must consider **sustainability**!" → {{T:Sustainable Development}} lineage.
- These imply different policies, different winners and losers, and different measures of success.
- → **The political core of development studies:** the choice of means is a distributional choice.

### 6.3 Question 3 — Why Is Development Important? (slide 17)
**Keywords:** justification, purpose, freedom from want, dignity, self-reliance, long-term survival

- Asks for the **justification** — what development is *for*, and why it is worth pursuing.
- Slide 18's answers span **civilisation and freedom from want**, **reduced surplus extraction**, **growth that works for the poor**, **global expansion and national pride**, **reaching the poorest and preventing "everyday" violence**, **long-term solutions for future generations**, and **de-centring power**.
- → Justification is what makes a theory persuasive. Two theories can prescribe similar policies for entirely different reasons — hence **one policy ≠ one theory**.

## 7. Each Answer Implies a Different Measure of Success
**Keywords:** metric, GDP, distribution, capabilities, intergenerational balance, evaluative stakes

The four answers on slide 16 double as four **measuring sticks** — a theory's metric reveals what it really values.

- **Growth** → GDP/GNP; prescribe industrialisation and investment ({{T:Modernisation}}, 8.1).
- **Equality** → distribution; prescribe equal trade and reduced surplus extraction ({{T:Dependency}}, 8.2).
- **Freedom** → capabilities and choice; prescribe meeting human needs ({{T:Human Development}}, 8.5).
- **Sustainability** → intergenerational balance; prescribe long-term planning ({{T:Sustainable Development}}, 8.6).
- → Because metrics decide who counts as a success, each *how* smuggles in a different *why* — theory choice is never purely technical.

## 8. The Seven Paradigms at a Glance (slide 18)
**Keywords:** comparative grid, paradigm families, course map, who/how/why

Slide 18 presents the course's master grid: seven paradigms, each answering **who needs development**, **how should it happen**, and **what is the importance**. This is the single most exam-relevant slide of Week 1 — Quiz 1 covers Weeks 2–5, and every one of these paradigms recurs.

### 8.1 {{T:Modernisation}}
**Keywords:** non-Western societies, mutually supportive arenas, foreign assistance, civilisation, material want

- **Who needs development:** traditional, un-industrialised societies — i.e. **non-Western countries**.
- **How:** modernise across **mutually supportive arenas**; rely on **foreign assistance**.
- **Why/importance:** a route to **civilisation** and **freedom from material want**.
- *Example:* {{A:Rostow 1960}}'s stages-of-growth model — any country could "take off" to self-sustaining growth if obstacles to modern business practice were overcome ({{A:Levitt 2021}}).
- → Key presupposition: development is a **universal linear path** that latecomers travel, with the West as the destination.

### 8.2 {{T:Dependency}}
**Keywords:** periphery, unequal exchange, technology transfer, surplus extraction, self-reliance

- **Who needs development:** **peripheral countries** — *Example:* Latin America.
- **How:** **equal trade relations**, **technology transfer**, and transformed **class relations**.
- **Why/importance:** **reduce surplus extraction and structural inequality**; achieve **self-reliance**.
- → Key move: underdevelopment is **not** an original condition to be outgrown but a **product of the same global process** that produced development elsewhere. Directly opposed to Modernisation's premise.

### 8.3 {{T:Neoliberalism}}
**Keywords:** structural adjustment, privatisation, deregulation, good governance, social capital, residual poverty

- **Who needs development:** **indebted and post-socialist states/countries reeling from structural adjustment**.
- **How:** **privatisation**; **free markets**; **financial deregulation**; **marketisation/controlled liberalisation**; **good governance**; **social capital**.
- **Why/importance:** **spur growth** and **promote individualism** / **make globalisation work for the poor**; **end residual poverty**.
- → Note **"residual"** poverty: the market is the presumed engine, and poverty is what remains for policy to mop up.

### 8.4 {{T:Developmental State}}
**Keywords:** East Asia, corporatism, entrepreneurial bureaucracy, export promotion, national pride

- **Who needs development:** **East Asian countries**.
- **How:** **corporatism**; **entrepreneurial bureaucracy**; **export promotion**.
- **Why/importance:** **expand into the global economy**; **preserve national pride**.
- → Explains the applied puzzle in Section 5: state direction of investment into strategic sectors while remaining export-oriented. Contrast with Neoliberalism — both embrace global markets, but only one makes the state entrepreneurial.

### 8.5 {{T:Human Development}}
**Keywords:** vulnerable individuals, human needs, holistic measures, everyday violence, freedom to choose

- **Who needs development:** **vulnerable and marginalised individuals** — note the shift of unit from *country* to *person*.
- **How:** **meeting human needs**; use **better, more holistic measures** of development (not GDP alone).
- **Why/importance:** **reach the poorest in society**; prevent **"everyday" violence** from being done; **freedom to pursue own choices**.
- → The "everyday violence" phrasing flags that deprivation is experienced continuously, not only in crises.

### 8.6 {{T:Sustainable Development}}
**Keywords:** current and future generations, growth vs degradation, long-term planning, mankind and environment

- **Who needs development:** **both the current and future generations** — the only paradigm that extends its constituency beyond the present.
- **How:** **strike a balance between growth and environmental degradation**; **long-term planning**; consideration of **both mankind and the environment**.
- **Why/importance:** a **future-oriented approach seeking long-term solutions**.
- → Unique move: it makes the **unborn** a stakeholder, which structurally limits present-day extraction.

### 8.7 {{T:Post-development}}
**Keywords:** subaltern actors, intellectual autonomy, grassroots mobilisation, rejection of modernity, de-centre power

- **Who needs development:** **subaltern actors** — *but also* **"the developed" themselves** (i.e. the paradigm refuses to treat development as a one-way lesson).
- **How:** **intellectual autonomy**; **local community empowerment**; **grassroots mobilisation**; **rejection of modernity** (as a Western-centric discourse).
- **Why/importance:** **avoid failures and homogenisation** of universal missions; **de-centre power**; a **participatory** approach to societal change.
- → Unlike the other six, this paradigm rejects the development project's own terms. It is the course's Week 9 destination, but it is flagged here at the start.

## 9. Reading the Grid: How to Use Slide 18
**Keywords:** comparison, cross-cutting patterns, policy implications, exam technique

- The grid is a **comparison device** — the kind the study-strategy guidance recommends building (slide 11; course outline).
- **Pattern 1 — the unit of concern shifts:** country (Modernisation, Dependency, Developmental State) → individual (Human Development) → generations (Sustainable Development) → actors within and beyond the "developed" (Post-development).
- **Pattern 2 — the state's role inverts:** entrepreneurial under the Developmental State; shrunk under Neoliberalism; ambivalent or suspect under Dependency and Post-development.
- **Pattern 3 — different theories, shared policy:** export promotion in both Developmental State and Neoliberal practice; redistributive language in both Dependency and Human Development — hence **one policy ≠ one theory**.
- → **Exam technique:** for any given policy, ask which *diagnosis* it presupposes before naming the paradigm. Naming the theory is half the mark; explaining how its presuppositions produce that policy is the other half.

## 10. Conclusion: The Two-Pronged Claim (slides 21–22)
**Keywords:** presupposition, comparative framing, corrigible flaws, revisable concept, theories and practices

The lecture closes with a two-part argument about what the concept of development *does*.

### 10.1 Prong 1 — Naming a Population "In Need" Is Never Neutral (slide 21)
**Keywords:** development exists elsewhere, causal account, comparative, diagnostic

- Identifying a population as in need of development **assumes two things**: that **development exists elsewhere** (the concept is inherently **comparative**), and that there is **some understanding of what caused that condition** (the label is also **causal**, carrying an implicit aetiology of poverty).
- → Disputes about who is "developing" are therefore also disputes about **the cause** of their condition, and thus about who bears responsibility for it.

### 10.2 Prong 2 — The Concept of the "Developed" Is Revisable (slide 21)
**Keywords:** social faults, correctable flaws, altered standard, normative opening

- Identifying social faults and inequalities and **viewing them as flaws that can be corrected** means the concept of what it means to be developed **can be altered**.
- → Development is **not a fixed yardstick** but a political and moral achievement that can be redefined — the opening that lets {{T:Human Development}}, {{T:Sustainable Development}} and {{T:Post-development}} claim the word for projects the classical paradigms would not recognise.

### 10.3 What Development Studies Is (slide 22)
**Keywords:** theories and discourses, practices and policies, Kothari, political implications

- {{A:Kothari 2019}}: {{D:development studies encompass a combination of theories or discourses, as well as practices and policies}} (slide 22).
- The field is **two-sided**: a body of theory **and** an apparatus of institutions, programmes and interventions.
- Closing question to the class: **how should we conceptualise development, and what political actions or interventions does this perspective imply?**
- → Every conceptualisation has a **policy entailment** — choosing a definition of development is choosing a politics.

## 11. Origins of the Development Agenda (Week 1 Essential Readings)
**Keywords:** decolonisation, Bandung 1955, UNCTAD 1964, NIEO, neoliberal turn, Kothari's critique

Context from the Week 1 essential readings ({{A:Kothari 2019}}; {{A:Levitt 2021}}) — part of two recommended textbooks plus essential and supplementary chapters, available through the CityU library (slide 13).

- {{A:Levitt 2021}}: the agenda came onto the table **before** the end of World War II, in anticipation of **decolonisation in Asia and Africa** — not in 1945 alone. Its founding literature came from independent-minded scholars across many countries — an international origin, later narrowed.
- Post-1945 the US priority was the **threat of communism**; a secondary aim was dissolving British and French preferential economic spheres.
- Counter-currents: **Bandung 1955** (Sukarno, Nehru, Nasser, Nkrumah) organised Asian–African **non-aligned** states against both Moscow and Washington; **UNCTAD** (1964, under {{A:Raúl Prebisch}}); the UN's **New International Economic Order** in the 1970s, to which the North conceded nothing meaningful.
- 1950s institutionalisation: the US State Department engaged leading universities to design development programmes (India → Harvard; Indonesia → MIT; Latin America → Chicago). {{T:Modernisation}} was the payoff — {{A:Rostow 1960}}'s *Stages of Economic Growth*, subtitled *A Non-Communist Manifesto*, dominated political science and sociology.
- **The turn (1979–80):** Thatcher and Reagan restored "the discipline of capital over labour" and cut back government in the developing world; a "doctrinal coup" at the World Bank dismissed the economists behind the **basic needs** approach and installed neoliberals. Southern industrialisation — which had matched industrialised-country growth rates in 1950–1980 — was reclassified as inefficient, and the founders of development economics were demonised as {{T:structuralist}}.
- → **Kothari's argument:** the standard periodisation (growth/modernisation → underdevelopment → neoliberalism/Washington Consensus, anchored on 1945) obscures a **longer genealogy**, hiding both continuities with colonialism and the critical discourses written out of mainstream accounts (Marxist, post-colonial, feminist).
- *Exam value:* {{A:Kothari 2019}} supplies Week 1's definition of development studies and the critique of how the field tells its history; {{A:Levitt 2021}} shows development was **contested and politically driven from the outset**, never neutral and technical.

## 13. One-Page Revision Spine
**Keywords:** last-minute recap, three questions, seven paradigms, assessment, study method

1. **Course aim:** not a step-by-step guide or a survey of every theory — it asks {{D:what constitutes development, and how we should understand development issues and their politics}} (slide 5).
