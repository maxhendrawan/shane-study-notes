## 2. The Puzzle in Miniature — Jane Smith and The Cause (section 1.1)
**Keywords:** Jane Smith, University of Colorado, affirmative action, picket, running example

### 2.1 The Thought Experiment — A Monday-Morning Picket (p. 3)
**Keywords:** diversity, discriminatory tenuring, quotas, The Cause, volunteers

- {{A:Jane Smith}}, a University of Colorado undergraduate, supports employment diversity, believes tenuring practices for minorities and women are discriminatory, and advocates affirmative action with quotas for protected classes.
- The Cause, a campus civil rights organization, shares her beliefs and program; it will picket The Administration next Monday at 9:00 A.M. and has called for volunteers.
- The question: will Jane join the demonstration, or wish the picketers well and stay home? *Example: this running example is revisited by every analytical move in the chapter.*
- It stands for the general question — why do individuals participate in collective dissent? — answered by general premises plus conditions unique to Jane.

### 2.2 The Two Key Definitions — Collective and Dissent
**Keywords:** collective, dissent, unconventional means, ill-defined boundary

- {{D:collective — individuals participate in some group activity.}}
- {{D:dissent — the individuals' objectives are to change government institutions, policies and/or personnel.}}
- Dissent's means are unconventional: beyond voting and interest-group activity — typically threatening and/or employing physical coercion and/or other illegal activities.
- The concept's boundaries "have always been ill defined" ({{A:Zimmermann 1983, chap. 2}}; {{A:Taylor and Jodice 1983, chap. 2}}); its core behaviours are the "negative cases" to theories of social order (preface, p. xi).

## 3. The Traditional Answer — the Deprived Actor (DA) Research Program (section 1.2, p. 4)
**Keywords:** Gurr 1970, deprivation, discontent, beliefs and attitudes

### 3.1 Core Claim and Mechanisms — Deprivation to Radicalism
**Keywords:** frustration, just deserts, equity, relative deprivation, alienation, cynicism, consciousness

- Foremost proponent {{A:Ted Gurr (1970)}}: {{D:deprivation produces discontent, and discontent in turn produces dissent}} — dissent is caused by preferences, beliefs, and attitudes towards personal deprivations.
- DA theories list cognitive and emotional processes that turn deprivations into dissent; Jane may join because she experiences one or more of:
- frustration over wants she cannot satisfy; lack of just deserts (felt entitlement vs what she got); lack of equity (inputs far exceed outputs); relative deprivation vis-à-vis advantaged groups;
- alienation from an illegitimate, unresponsive government; political cynicism and lack of trust; class or racial consciousness; radicalism and identification with dissident groups ({{A:Zimmermann 1983, chap. 4}}).
- *Example: relative deprivation needs no absolute misery — a group whose expectations outrun its achievements, or whose position improves then reverses, feels more deprived than one always poor.*

### 3.2 The Expected Causal Chain and Lichbach's Verdict — "Seems Beyond Dispute"
**Keywords:** grievances, identification, anger, rhetorical question

- The chain: deprivations → grievances about patterns of authority → identification with similarly deprived actors → anger → behavioural dissent.
- Applied to Jane: severe discrimination → profound grievances → deep sympathy for The Cause → angry enough to join the demonstration.
- Lichbach grants the specific processes might be questioned but says the general DA perspective "seems beyond dispute"; his rhetorical question sets up the challenge — is it not obvious that deprived people become frustrated, the frustrated angry, and the angry often strike out?

## 4. The Challenge — the Collective Action (CA) Research Program (section 1.3, p. 5)
**Keywords:** Olson 1965, Tullock 1971a, motivation disputed, PG-PD

### 4.1 Olson and Tullock — The Logic of Collective Action and the Paradox of Revolution
**Keywords:** formalisation, assumptions challenged, research program

- First formalised in {{A:Mancur Olson's (1965)}} {{T:The Logic of Collective Action}}; the most-cited application to protest is {{A:Gordon Tullock's (1971a)}} "{{T:The Paradox of Revolution}}."
- *Example: Tullock's paradox — one rebel's participation can never decide the revolution's outcome, so self-interest says stay home, yet revolutions happen.*
- Both dispute the assumptions DA theories make about rebel motivation, and so dispute that individual deprivation leads to collective dissent; Lichbach calls this the {{T:CA research program}}.

### 4.2 Jane's Problem in Rational-Choice Terms — Exhibit 1, "Jane's Dilemma" (p. 5)
**Keywords:** Demonstrate or Stay Home, Everyone Else, 2x2 matrix, four outcomes

- Jane chooses Demonstrate (D) or Stay Home (S), weighing outcomes against what she expects Everyone Else to do; the four possibilities form Exhibit 1, "Jane's Dilemma" (p. 5); (D,S) = Jane Demonstrates while Everyone Else Stays Home.
- (D,D) all Demonstrate: benefits = Diversity, costs = Time Lost. (S,D) Jane alone stays home: benefits = Diversity, costs = 0 — The Cause's loss of strength is imperceptible.
- (S,S) all Stay Home: benefits = 0, costs = 0. (D,S) Jane alone demonstrates: benefits = 0, costs = Time Lost.
- Exam-friendly (memorise): Diversity comes if Everyone Else demonstrates; Jane pays Time Lost only when she Demonstrates.
- *Example: Lichbach's aside — campus police are unkind to Jane's solo picket of the dean's office, much kinder when hundreds of students block it.*

### 4.3 Normative vs Positive Analysis, and Three Labels — PD, PG, Free Riding
**Keywords:** mutual cooperation, dominant strategy, public goods, non-excludability, free riding

- {{D:Normative analysis: all Demonstrate is better for Jane and Everyone Else than all Stay Home — mutual cooperation is in every member's interest.}}
- {{D:Positive analysis: whatever Jane expects others to do, she prefers to Stay Home.}} If others Demonstrate, benefits come anyway and protest is costly; if others Stay Home, benefits are unavailable anyway and protest is still costly — her beliefs about others are irrelevant.
- So all Stay Home, no one Demonstrates, The Administration is safe, and the dean conducts business as usual.
- {{T:Prisoner's Dilemma (PD)}}: {{D:the normative and positive analyses conflict — mutual cooperation is better for everyone, yet individualistically rational behaviour produces mutual noncooperation.}} *Example: two suspects interrogated separately both confess, though both would gain by silence; Jane's picket is the many-player version.*
- {{T:public goods (PG) problem}}: {{D:costs are private and high (time, money, injury risk), while the benefit of success can be enjoyed by anyone regardless of participation.}} *Example: like national defence or clean air, Jane enjoys an end to discrimination even if she never pickets.*
- Hence each sympathiser wants to {{T:free ride}}: {{D:everyone hopes that someone else will absorb the costs, yet everyone wishes to share in the benefits.}}

### 4.4 The Odds Stacked Against Dissent — Naming the Two Dilemmas
**Keywords:** low probability, independence, opportunity costs, Cooperators' Dilemma, Rebel's Dilemma

- "All of the cards seem stacked against large-scale protests and rebellions": a revolt's success is low-probability, and both success and benefits are almost independent of any individual's participation.
- Costs are certain and private: rebels have many competing causes and demands on their time (e.g., forgone wages), and participation is often costly and dangerous — governments maim or murder their enemies.
- → Balance sheet (memorise): benefits are uncertain, shared, and independent of any individual; costs are certain, private, and sometimes fatal — so rebellion, not quiescence, is what needs explaining.
- *Example: a lobby of a few firms organises and wins protection; a latent group of millions of consumers or taxpayers, each with a negligible stake, does not.*
- Unless the CA problem is overcome, rational people will never rebel — rebellions would require irrationality. {{D:Cooperators' Dilemma — the general tragedy of all participants in collective action (Lichbach forthcoming).}} {{D:Rebel's Dilemma — the specific tragedy of dissenters.}}

## 5. Why the Puzzle Matters — The Empirical Paradox
**Keywords:** prediction vs observation, 95 per cent, central question, opposite problematiques

### 5.1 The Empirical Paradox and the Two Categories of People — The 95 Per Cent Case
**Keywords:** aggrieved nonrebels, those who rebel, silent majority

- The CA argument predicts rational people will not rebel, yet people quite often rebel, sometimes in very large numbers — the {{T:paradox or puzzle of CA}}.
- Category 1: the aggrieved who do not rebel — by far the larger group: at least ninety-five per cent of aggrieved people, at least ninety-five per cent of the time, in at least ninety-five per cent of places. Critics ignore it, yet it fits Olson.
- Category 2: those who do rebel — the smaller group, but some do overcome the Rebel's Dilemma; this is the puzzle.
- *Example: the 95 per cent figure is an order-of-magnitude claim, not a statistic — it names the silent-majority case that a theory of rebellion must explain rather than explain away.*

### 5.2 The Central Question and Opposite Problematiques — Gurr vs Olson
**Keywords:** cooperation, sacrifice, unity, selective incentives

- The book's central question: How is the Rebel's Dilemma solved? Under what circumstances will cooperation emerge? What induces an individual to make a sacrifice to obtain a PG?
- DA theorists, Marxists, Weberians, and agenda setters all seek the same thing from their own perspectives: the causes of political unity — what lets groups form and people defend common interests.
- Opposite problematiques: given ubiquitous grievances, {{A:Gurr (1970)}} must explain {{D:why people do not rebel}}; given ubiquitous free riding, {{A:Olson (1965)}} must explain {{D:why people do rebel}} — via selective incentives and federal group solutions.

## 6. First Reception — Applied Students of Collective Dissent (section 1.4, p. 7)
**Keywords:** the marriage, sociologists, political scientists, economists, largely failed

### 6.1 The Marriage Brokers — General Borrowings, Not the Rebel's Dilemma
**Keywords:** rational action, bargaining, nonelites, Tarrow

- Students of conflict tried to wed CA ideas to conflict studies; the "marriage brokers" were sociologists, political scientists, and economists who drew on the general rational-actor argument, not the specific dilemma.
- Political violence was redefined in rational-action terms ({{A:Zimmermann 1983, 603–4, fn. 47}}) and as bargaining: "acts of disruption, destruction, injury whose purpose, choice of targets or victims, surrounding circumstances, implementation, and/or effects have political significance..." ({{A:Nieburg 1969, 13}}).
- Nonelites too calculate costs and benefits ({{A:Armstrong 1976, 394}}); {{A:Tarrow (1989, 8, 16)}} — people protest when other routes to their interests are less productive, or gains outweigh costs. Widely cited: {{A:Oberschall (1973)}}, {{A:Zald and McCarthy (1990)}}, {{A:Korpi (1974)}}, {{A:Gamson (1990)}}, {{A:Tilly (1978)}}, {{A:Blalock (1989)}}.

### 6.2 Tilly (1978) — Collective Rationality as Evasion
**Keywords:** group benefits vs group costs, Marxist question, Adam Smith and Karl Marx

- {{A:Tilly (1978)}} rejects individual rationality for {{T:collective rationality}} — participation when the group's benefits exceed the group's costs — guided by the Marxist question of group conflict, not the CA question; his model is Adam Smith plus Karl Marx.
- As {{A:Gamson (1990, 138)}} puts it: "{{D:The collective goals of political actors rather than the personal goals of members are assumed to be the relevant part of an explanation of political behavior.}}"
- He never explains how the Rebel's Dilemma is solved — an alternative, not a solution; Gamson and Zald & McCarthy proceed likewise, borrowing only selective incentives and entrepreneurs.
- → One-liner: changing the subject from individual to group rationality is evasion, not solution — the Rebel's Dilemma is never answered.

### 6.3 Economists, the Two Negative Consequences, and the Verdict — "The Marriage ... Has Largely Failed"
**Keywords:** Olson's Problem, expected utility, Marwell and Oliver, Mueller, theory vs substance

- Economists split into models that do **not** confront Olson's Problem (externalities and PGs {{A:Olson 1969}}; positional goods {{A:Shubik 1971}}; contract-curve tradeoffs {{A:Hirshleifer 1987}}; regime and opposition calculus {{A:Lichbach 1984, 1987}}; {{A:Wintrobe 1990}}; state-opposition games {{A:Sandler, Tschirhart, and Cauley 1983}}; consociational institutions {{A:Tsebelis 1990}});
- and models that **do** capture his insights ({{A:Lichbach 1992, 345–47}}): binary expected-utility models ({{A:Tullock 1971a}}; {{A:DeNardo 1985}}; {{A:Muller and Opp 1986}}; {{A:Opp 1989}}), continuous models (riots, hijacking, terrorism, revolution — {{A:Landes 1978}}; {{A:Sandler and Scott 1987}}; {{A:Mueller 1989, 173–75}}), and PD models ({{A:Buchanan 1979}}). These take the PG-PD problem seriously: without selective incentives, a rational person will ordinarily not rebel.
- Two negative consequences followed: the bulk of CA ideas was ignored, so its explanatory potential was never exploited; and the bulk of its implications for collective dissent went unexplored.
- {{A:Marwell and Oliver (1984, 3)}}: "Although Olson's work has thus had a general impact on social movement theory, *most* of his analysis is only marginally considered in that literature"; (1984, 2): "Everyone assumes that collective action and social movements have a close relationship, but the nature of that relationship remains ill-defined."
- Verdict: "The marriage between the CA research program and conflict studies, in short, has largely failed" — sociologists and political scientists ignored the theory, economists the substance; the contrast with Gurr, who derived integrated propositions about domestic conflict, is stark.
- Economists agree: {{A:Mueller (1989, 175)}} — the rational-behaviour approach "does not generate a rich harvest of testable implications"; {{A:Eggertsson (1990, 73–74)}} — Tullock's theory of revolution shows the limits of the purely economic approach where free riding pays. → One-liner: the theorists never applied their theory; the applied scholars never used it.

### 6.4 Olson's Own Inconsistent Position — Waffling on Mass Movements
**Keywords:** amoral familism, utopia, "lunatic fringe", later confidence

- {{A:Olson (1965, 161–62)}} apparently believes "mass movements" are better explained by social psychological and collective behaviour theories than by his own — but he waffles.
- Confident side: mass movements are "usually not very massive" (p. 162); his theory fits the {{T:amoral familism}} culture discovered by {{A:Banfield (1958)}} (p. 162, fn. 97); utopia could be rational because "if the benefit that would come from establishing a utopia is infinite, it could be rational even for the member of a large group to contribute voluntarily..." (p. 161, fn. 94).
- *Example: the utopia case is the one case Olson concedes can defeat his logic — a group so large that no individual contribution matters still mobilises if the payoff is treated as infinite.*
- Most ironically, Olson is confident that an alternative approach — DA-style explanation by "alienation", fanatic devotion to an ideology or leader, and the "lunatic fringe" — works where his own fails; he later regained confidence ({{A:Olson 1990, 1991a–d}}).

## 7. Second Reception — Pure Theorists of Collective Action (section 1.5, p. 11)
**Keywords:** the happier story, four assumptions, two dozen solutions, irony

### 7.1 The Happier Story and the Four Assumptions — Utility Maximizers to No Impact
**Keywords:** obvious challenge, two categories, PG demands, very large group, individual irrelevance

- While conflict scholars met Olson with disinterest, CA theorists busily elaborated his argument — "the happier story"; the obvious challenge is that Olson is simply wrong (people do rebel, often in large numbers).
- CA analysis rests on four assumptions: (1) individuals are utility maximizers evaluating outcomes of possible actions; (2) demands are typically for a PG; (3) the group of potential sympathisers is very large (thousands); (4) large groups can affect policy, but each individual's participation has no discernible impact on success.

### 7.2 Solving the Dilemma, and Lichbach's Closing Summary — Revise the Assumptions and Remedy the Field
**Keywords:** more than two tactics, uncertainty, more than two dozen solutions, next chapter

- The dilemma is solved by revising the assumptions: widening the choice beyond two tactics (staying home, voting, lobbying, demonstrating), allowing more than two expectations about others, and introducing uncertainty about outcomes.
- Most CA theorists accordingly propose free-rider solutions — modifying the basic PG-PD game ({{A:Lichbach forthcoming, chap. 2}}); there are presently more than two dozen distinct solutions to {{T:Olson's Problem}} (Exhibit 2.1, p. 21). *Example: once Jane's choice set widens beyond {D, S}, the 2x2 game no longer describes her decision and a solution becomes possible.*
- Closing summary: the marriage of CA theory with conflict studies failed — few ideas used, few issues studied — which is ironic given the two dozen solutions; the book's purpose is to remedy this and bring the CA program's richness to bear on conflict studies. The next chapter outlines the approach.

## 8. Where the Book Goes Next — The Four Families of Solutions (from the book's contents)
**Keywords:** table of contents, provenance, Market, Community, Contract, Hierarchy

- Provenance: from the table of contents bound with Chapter 1 — orientation only, not chapter content; Chapter 1 promises the survey, later parts deliver it.
- {{T:Market}} — increase benefits; lower costs (no-cost, low-cost, opportunity, fixed); increase resources; improve the productivity of tactics (production function, innovations, violence vs nonviolence); reduce the PG's supply; raise the probability of winning and of making a difference; use incomplete information (publicity, illusions, ideology, symbols; control of information); increase risk taking; team competition between enemies; restrict exit; change the type of PG (public bads, nonrival PGs).
- {{T:Community}} — common knowledge (mutual expectations; bandwagon: temporal and spatial diffusion) and common values (process orientation; other-regardingness).
- {{T:Contract}} — types (self-government; tit-for-tat; mutual exchange) and origins (longevity, homogeneity, preexisting organization, autonomy, stability, concentration).
- {{T:Hierarchy}} — entrepreneurs and patrons; reorganise (club, efficacious group, federal structure); team competition among allies; impose, monitor, enforce agreements — selective incentives and disincentives.
- Themes and appraisal: Chapter 7, Solutions as Politics (origins; the Rebel's Dilemma vs the State's Dilemma); Chapter 8, Solutions as Pathologies (unintended consequences; the Iron Laws); Chapter 9 evaluates CA theories (intersolution comparison; predictability — grievances, preference falsification; Lakatos and Popper applied to the four families); Chapter 10 improves them (deepen the micro; explore the macro); Chapter 11, A Final Perspective. Exhibits: 1.1 Jane's Dilemma (p. 5); 2.1 Solutions (p. 21).

## 9. Lichbach's Own Position and Project
**Keywords:** anger is not enough, Tilly's evasion, rich program unused, remedial and evaluative

- What he rejects: that deprivation's anger suffices — the Rebel's Dilemma must be solved for rebellion to occur; Tilly's collective rationality, which never says how; and both halves of the failed marriage — conflict scholars ignored the theory, economists the substance.
- His diagnosis: the CA program is rich (more than two dozen PG-PD solutions) and conflict studies has not used it.
- His project: explore the CA program's implications for the causes, courses, and consequences of collective dissent and evaluate it in {{A:Lakatos's (1970)}} and {{A:Popper's (1968)}} senses; his contribution in this chapter is naming the {{T:Cooperators' Dilemma}} and the {{T:Rebel's Dilemma}}.
- The question the book pursues: How is the Rebel's Dilemma solved? The approach: modify the basic PG-PD assumptions and survey the solutions (Chapter 2, "The Approach Adopted").

## 10. Working Vocabulary and Key Names
**Keywords:** definition bank, research programs, dilemma vocabulary, named scholars

### 10.1 The Research Programs and the Dilemma Vocabulary — DA, CA, PD, PG
**Keywords:** deprived actor, collective action, problematique, free rider, Olson's Problem

- {{D:DA (Deprived Actor) — the traditional approach: deprivation produces discontent, discontent produces dissent (foremost proponent Gurr 1970).}}
- {{D:CA (Collective Action) — the rational-choice approach inaugurated by Olson (1965), disputing that deprivation leads to dissent.}}
- {{D:problematique — the guiding question of a research program.}} Gurr's: why do people not rebel? Olson's: why do people rebel?
- {{D:PD (Prisoner's Dilemma) — individually rational behaviour produces mutual noncooperation even though mutual cooperation is better for everyone.}}
- {{D:PG (public goods) problem — the benefit of a successful protest can be enjoyed by anyone regardless of participation.}}
- {{D:free rider — someone who hopes others will absorb the costs while still sharing the benefits.}}
- {{D:PG-PD game — Lichbach's shorthand for the basic model CA theorists modify.}}
- {{D:Cooperators' Dilemma — the general tragedy of all participants in CA.}} {{D:Rebel's Dilemma — the specific tragedy of dissenters.}}
- {{D:Olson's Problem — how the free-rider problem is overcome; the question Olson leaves unanswered: why people do rebel.}}
- {{D:Olson's General Law of Collective Action — large groups tend to fail to organise for collective goods; deviations from it are what CA theorists explain.}}
- {{D:the paradox or puzzle of CA — people do rebel, sometimes in very large numbers, despite the CA logic.}}

### 10.2 Solutions, Models, and Names — Selective Incentives to Lakatos and Popper
**Keywords:** selective incentives, federal group solutions, amoral familism, expected utility

- {{D:selective incentives — the standard Olson solution to the free-rider problem.}} {{D:federal group solutions — Olson's other named solution.}}
- {{D:amoral familism — the culture discovered by Banfield (1958) that Olson (1965, 162, fn. 97) says his theory fits.}}
- {{D:expected utility models — dissent modelled in binary form (dissent vs nondissent) or continuous form (a continuous level of contributions).}}
- {{D:intersolution comparison and predictability — the analytical and empirical criteria for evaluating CA theories (Chapter 9).}}
- {{D:collective dissent — group activity to change government institutions, policies and/or personnel, by means beyond voting and interest-group activity.}}
- Foundational works: {{A:Olson (1965)}}; {{A:Tullock (1971a)}}; {{A:Gurr (1970)}}; {{A:Tilly (1978)}}; {{A:Gamson (1990)}}; {{A:Marwell and Oliver (1984)}}. Method: {{A:Lakatos (1970)}}, {{A:Popper (1968)}}; Lichbach's own: {{A:Lichbach (1984, 1987, 1990, 1992, forthcoming)}}.
